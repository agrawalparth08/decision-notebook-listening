It is the third week after launch, and the founder of a small cab booking app is sitting alone in the office at eleven at night, staring at a dashboard that refuses to move. Fourteen drivers have registered across the whole city. Twenty nine riders have downloaded the app. And almost nobody is completing a ride, because the arithmetic underneath the product is brutal in a way none of the pitch deck slides had prepared her for. A rider opens the app on a Tuesday evening, sees no cars anywhere near her street, closes the app, and does not open it again for a week, maybe longer, maybe never. A driver logs on for two hours, receives no ride requests because there are barely thirty riders in a city of four million people, and decides the app is not worth the data charges, let alone the time. The product itself is not broken. The screens work, the payments clear, the map loads correctly. The problem sits somewhere the engineering team cannot fix with a sprint, because the problem is that a marketplace with almost nobody on either side is not really a marketplace at all, it is two empty rooms with a locked door between them, and no amount of polish on the door changes that.

Her investor, on a call the next morning, says something that annoys her at first and then will not leave her head for the rest of the week. He tells her she is trying to sell eggs before she owns a single chicken. What she actually needs to do, he says, is stop thinking like a product company and start thinking like an economist standing between two markets that do not yet trust each other to show up. Somebody has to move first, and moving first is expensive, and whoever pays that cost is not paying for software, they are paying to manufacture the very thing the business will eventually sell, which is not rides at all, it is the confidence that the other side of the market will be there when you need them.

She spends the next month doing something that feels, on paper, like throwing money away. She pays drivers a flat guarantee for every hour they are logged on and available, whether or not a single ride request arrives, funded almost entirely out of the seed round meant to last eighteen months. Riders barely notice the change. Drivers do. Within six weeks, driver supply in the two densest neighbourhoods of the city crosses a rough threshold, enough cars circulating that a rider opening the app at almost any hour sees three or four vehicles within a five minute pickup. Word starts moving between riders the way it always does, not through advertising, but through one person telling another that the app actually works now. The dashboard she had stared at for three weeks starts climbing on its own, and it does not stop climbing when she eventually pulls the driver guarantee back six months later, because by then the network had learned to feed itself.

Now here is the one thread that runs underneath every framework in this course, worth stating plainly before we touch a single model. Most of what you have learned in other courses this year concerns products that have a fixed, physical value regardless of who else owns one. A kilogram of rice, a hotel room, a bar of soap, these things are worth roughly what they are worth whether you are the only buyer in the market or the millionth. Information systems break that assumption in a specific and important way. A great many of the products and platforms this course studies are worth more, not less, as more people use them, and that single reversal changes almost everything about how you compete, how you price, how you decide what to build first, and how you should read a competitor's seemingly irrational behaviour. The course material on networks and the course material on how organisations actually use, misuse, and fail to use their information systems are really two halves of the same coin. One half asks how digital value gets created in the first place, through connection rather than production. The other half asks the much more sobering question of why so many organisations, having correctly identified a valuable system to build, then fail to get any value out of it once it is built. Keep both halves in view as we go, because a system can win the market and still lose the company, if nobody inside the company changes a single decision because of it.

Let us begin with the idea that explains almost everything strange about digital businesses, the framework the entire cab app story was secretly built around. Network effects.

In plain words, most products carry their value inside themselves. A network product carries its value in the other people who also own it. One person holding a telephone owns, in a very real sense, a brick, an expensive, well engineered object that does nothing useful on its own, because there is nobody on the other end of the line. Everyone in a country owning a telephone owns something entirely different, they own a country, a web of reachable people, and the value of any single phone in that web is not a property of the phone itself, it is a property of everyone else's decision to also own one. This is the defining feature of a network good, and it means the value curve for these products does not simply rise as adoption rises, it can rise explosively, because every new user adds one more connection to every single existing user simultaneously, not merely one more node to the network.

The everyday version of this idea is something you have felt without naming it. Think about a messaging app that nobody in your friend circle uses yet. It does not matter how elegant its interface is, how fast its servers are, how clever its stickers are, it is close to worthless to you, because there is nobody to message. Now think about the messaging app that every single person you know already has installed. Even if a rival app is objectively better designed, switching costs you something real, because switching means asking dozens of people to follow you somewhere new, and most of them will not bother. The value was never really in the app. It was in the country of people already standing inside it.

The business example with numbers is the founder's cab app story we opened with, and it is worth naming the arithmetic explicitly. With fourteen drivers and twenty nine riders scattered across a city of four million, the probability that any single rider, opening the app at a random moment, sees an available car nearby is close to zero, and a near zero probability of a good outcome produces a near zero rate of repeat usage, which is precisely why the app felt broken even though every line of code worked correctly. The subsidy that fixed it was not marketing spend in the ordinary sense, it was the direct manufacturing of network density, buying enough driver supply, concentrated in dense neighbourhoods, that the probability of a nearby car crossed the threshold where riders actually experienced the product working. Once that threshold was crossed in two neighbourhoods, the network there began generating its own new users through word of mouth, at effectively zero additional cost per user, which is the entire economic logic that makes early, expensive subsidies rational for network businesses in a way they would be irrational for a business selling, say, bars of soap.

When do you reach for this framework. Any time you are evaluating a business, or building one, where the product's usefulness to any one user depends on how many other users are already present, messaging, marketplaces, payment networks, social platforms, even enterprise software where colleagues need to be on the same tool to collaborate. The signal to watch for is whether the product gets meaningfully better for existing users purely because new users joined, with no change to the product itself. If the answer is yes, you are looking at a network good, and ordinary competitive intuition, the assumption that the best product wins, needs to be replaced with a different question, which product accumulates the most users fastest, because scale itself becomes a form of quality that a slower, even objectively superior rival struggles to overcome.

So remember. A product with no network around it is a brick, however well made. A product with a country of users around it earns its value from that country, not from itself alone, and this is exactly why network businesses spend so heavily, and seemingly irrationally, to win users early, because they are not buying customers, they are manufacturing the very asset the business is built on.

Pause here and try this yourself. A food delivery app in a mid sized city has decent restaurant coverage but very few riders signed up to deliver, and delivery times are consistently long, closer to an hour than the thirty minutes customers expect. Using the network effects lens, what is the app actually short of, and would you expect a marketing campaign aimed at customers, or a subsidy aimed at delivery riders, to fix the underlying problem faster.

Here is the answer talked through. The long delivery time is not a customer acquisition problem in disguise, it is a symptom of an undersupplied side of the network, too few riders relative to the order volume the restaurants can generate, which means every additional customer the marketing campaign brings in will experience the same disappointing hour long wait and may churn immediately, potentially making word of mouth worse rather than better. A subsidy aimed specifically at recruiting and retaining delivery riders addresses the actual bottleneck directly, shortening delivery times for the existing customer base first, which is what would make any subsequent customer marketing spend actually pay off, because new customers arriving into a network that already works will have a good first experience instead of a disappointing one.

Now the second framework, and it is the one that explains why some of the largest companies in the world today own almost nothing they sell. Platform versus pipeline.

In plain words, a pipeline business is the traditional shape of a company. It makes a thing, or provides a service directly, and value flows in one direction, from the company to the customer, in exchange for money flowing the other way. A hotel chain owns its beds. It built them, or bought them, and it profits by renting them out night after night. A platform business does something structurally different. It does not own the inventory being exchanged at all. It owns the connection between two sides of a market, and it profits by taking a small toll on every match it successfully makes. The booking app that lets you reserve a room in that same hotel, or a car from a driver across town, owns neither the beds nor the cars. It owns something less tangible and, in the right conditions, far more valuable, which is the trust and convenience of the matching itself.

The everyday version of this distinction is the difference between a shop that sells umbrellas and a matchmaker who introduces umbrella sellers to people caught in the rain. The shop has to buy inventory, store it, manage the risk of umbrellas going unsold, and its growth is capped by how much inventory it can afford to hold. The matchmaker holds no umbrellas at all, faces no inventory risk, and can, in principle, scale to match an unlimited number of buyers and sellers, limited only by how good the matching itself becomes, and how many people trust the matchmaker enough to use them instead of buying directly.

The business example with numbers returns us to the cab and hotel booking comparison directly, and it is worth being specific about why platforms so often outgrow the pipeline businesses sitting right next to them in the same market. A hotel chain that wants to double its room count must build or acquire twice as many physical rooms, a capital intensive process that takes years and carries real construction and financing risk on the company's own balance sheet. A booking platform that wants to double the room nights it can offer travellers needs only to convince twice as many independently owned hotels and homeowners to list their existing rooms on the platform, a process that is far cheaper, far faster, and decisively does not require the platform to raise a single rupee of construction capital, because the inventory being added already exists, owned by somebody else, sitting idle some nights of the year. This is precisely why platform businesses can, in the right market, out scale the pipeline incumbents they compete with, they are renting other people's assets rather than building their own, and the growth constraint shifts from capital availability to trust and matching quality instead.

But platforms carry a structural weakness that pure pipelines never face, and this is the chicken and egg problem the cab app story lived through directly. A ride hailing platform is worthless to a rider if there are no drivers, and worthless to a driver if there are no riders, and unlike a pipeline business, which can simply build one side of its operation, inventory, and start selling, a platform must somehow solve both sides simultaneously, or convince one side to show up and wait for the other. The standard, repeatedly proven solution is asymmetric subsidy, identifying whichever side of the market is more price sensitive, or more essential to the other side's experience, and effectively paying that side to show up first, absorbing a real and sometimes substantial loss, until the network reaches a density where it can sustain itself through organic word of mouth rather than continued subsidy.

When do you reach for this framework. Whenever you are assessing any business that sits between two groups who need to find each other, buyers and sellers, riders and drivers, employers and job seekers, landlords and tenants. Ask first whether the company genuinely owns the underlying asset being exchanged, in which case it is a pipeline and its growth will be capital constrained, or whether it merely facilitates the exchange of assets owned by others, in which case it is a platform and its growth will be constrained by trust, matching quality, and the difficulty of solving cold start on both sides at once. And whenever you see a platform business burning cash aggressively on one side of its market in its early years, do not assume recklessness, ask instead which side is being subsidised, and what that reveals about which side the founders believe is scarcer or more price sensitive.

So remember. A pipeline owns its inventory and grows as fast as it can build; a platform owns the connection and can grow faster, renting assets it never had to buy, but only after it survives the chicken and egg problem, usually by subsidising whichever side of the market is more sensitive to price until the network can stand on its own.

Now a framework that sits right next to platform thinking, because it explains one of the most disorienting moves a competitor can make against you, which is giving away, for free, forever, the exact product you are charging money for.

In plain words, when a rival offers your core product for nothing, the instinctive response is to assume they are either losing money on purpose to buy market share temporarily, or that they have simply not figured out how to monetise yet. Sometimes that is true. But the more dangerous version, the one worth training yourself to recognise immediately, is when the rival has deliberately concluded that your entire product category should not be a business at all, because they intend to make their money somewhere else in the value chain, somewhere your business model has no presence.

The everyday version of this is the free newspaper handed out at a train station, funded entirely by advertising, competing against a newspaper you pay for at a stand. The paid newspaper's owner might reasonably assume the free paper is a lower quality product being subsidised temporarily, and wait for it to run out of money. But if the free paper's owner has actually built a durable advertising business that scales with readership regardless of what readers pay, the free paper is not a temporary discount, it is a permanent, structurally different business, and waiting it out is a losing strategy.

The business example with numbers is the case of a Chinese security software company, which we will return to in fuller detail shortly, but the shape of it belongs here, inside the framework. An entire antivirus software industry had built itself around charging users an annual subscription fee for protection software, a business model that had held for years and generated meaningful, predictable revenue for the incumbents. A new entrant offered antivirus protection completely free, permanently, to every user, effectively destroying the revenue model the entire industry depended on almost overnight. The entrant was not losing money by accident. It had identified that the antivirus software, once installed on tens of millions of machines, gave it something the subscription fee never could, direct, trusted, default access to the browser and the app store on every one of those machines, and it monetised that access instead, through browser defaults and app distribution deals, a revenue stream with no connection whatsoever to the price of antivirus software itself.

When do you reach for this framework. The moment a competitor's pricing looks irrational to you, resist the urge to assume incompetence or temporary subsidy, and instead ask a sharper question, what does this competitor gain simply from having my product installed, trusted, or in daily use, separate entirely from what they charge for it. If you can identify a plausible answer, you are not looking at a price war, you are looking at a different business model entirely, wearing your product's clothes, and matching their price without matching their model is a slow way to lose.

So remember. When a rival gives away your core product for free, they are very often not competing with your product at all, they are competing with your business model, and the correct response is to find your own second source of value, not to simply lower your price and hope the fight stays on the old battlefield.

Now the fourth framework, and it is the one this course insists on above almost every other, because it is the discipline that separates thoughtful technology leaders from people chasing whatever word is loudest in the industry that year. The right tool test.

In plain words, every new technology arrives with an army of advocates convinced it changes everything, and the discipline this course teaches is to resist that army until you have answered one specific, unglamorous question, what decision or transaction does this actually change, and is there a cheaper, more boring tool that changes it just as well. The blockchain conversation in class made this concrete, because blockchain is expensive, genuinely expensive, in computing power, in complexity, in the coordination it demands from every participant, and that expense only earns its keep under one specific condition, when the parties involved genuinely cannot trust any single central party to keep an honest record on everyone's behalf.

The everyday version of the test is a group of friends splitting a dinner bill. Nobody needs a distributed, tamper proof, cryptographically secured ledger to remember that one friend owes another six hundred rupees. A phone note, or even just memory, works perfectly well, because the friends trust each other, or at worst trust whichever one of them is doing the maths, well enough for the stakes involved. Reaching for blockchain here is not innovation, it is theatre, solving a trust problem that does not actually exist with a tool built specifically for situations where it does.

The business example with numbers is the deliberate contrast the course draws between that dinner and a genuinely fitting use case, rival banks settling trades with each other. Here, no single bank trusts a competing bank's internal ledger as the final word on who owes whom, and no bank wants to hand a competitor's system the authority to be the single source of truth either, which is precisely the condition under which a distributed ledger, expensive as it is, earns its cost, because the alternative is not a simpler solution, it is a slower, more expensive web of manual reconciliation and dispute resolution between mutually distrustful institutions. The technology's cost stops being a drawback and becomes the price of removing a trust problem that had no other honest solution.

When do you reach for this framework. Every single time a new technology arrives in a meeting with momentum behind it and nobody has yet asked what decision it changes. Ask plainly, is there a central party here that every participant could, in principle, trust to keep the record honestly. If yes, a database, an unglamorous, well understood, comparatively cheap tool, is very likely the right answer, and this rule generalises past blockchain entirely, to every technology wave that arrives with evangelists attached, because the question, what specific decision does this change, and what is the cheapest tool that changes it, never goes out of date, even as the fashionable technology at the centre of the question does.

So remember. The right tool test has one question at its centre: does a central, trustworthy keeper of records genuinely not exist here. If one does, reach for the cheap, boring database, because friends splitting dinner do not need a blockchain, and most business problems, however excitingly they are described in a vendor's pitch deck, are closer to a dinner bill than to rival banks settling trades.

Pause here and try this yourself. A hospital group is considering whether to build a blockchain system to track patient records across its five branches, all owned by the same parent company, all already required by regulation to share data honestly with each other. Using the right tool test, what question should the technology committee ask first, and what would you expect the honest answer to point toward.

Here is the answer talked through. The first question is whether there exists a central party here that every branch could, in principle, trust to keep an honest shared record, and since all five branches belong to the same parent company, already operate under the same regulatory obligation to share data honestly, and have no adversarial relationship with each other the way rival banks or unrelated business partners might, the honest answer very likely points toward a well designed, properly secured, shared database, not a blockchain, because the expensive property blockchain buys you, the ability to maintain trust and an honest record without any central authority, is not actually needed when a trustworthy central authority, the parent company itself, already exists and already has the obligation and incentive to keep the system honest.

Now the final framework of the technical section, and it is the one the course material presses hardest, because it explains why so many technically successful information systems projects still end up being organisational failures. The discipline of measuring adoption, not launch, and valuing systems by the decisions they change.

In plain words, the research on large information technology projects, studied across many companies and many years, keeps finding the same three failure patterns, almost regardless of industry or the specific technology involved. First, requirements get frozen far too early in the process, with the people who will actually use the system consulted too little, and too briefly, before specifications are locked in and the building begins. Second, these systems are typically deployed all at once, a single big bang cutover across the whole organisation, rather than phased in gradually where problems could surface cheaply, on a small scale, and be fixed before they become expensive and organisation wide. Third, and perhaps most damaging, success gets measured at the moment of launch, whether the system technically works, whether the go live date was hit, rather than months later, at the moment that actually matters, whether people are genuinely using the system to make different, better decisions than they made before it existed.

The everyday version of this failure pattern is buying an expensive piece of home gym equipment, assembling it correctly, placing it in a prominent spot in the house, and considering the project a complete success the day it is set up and functioning, without ever checking, six months later, whether anyone is actually using it or whether it has quietly become an expensive place to hang laundry. The equipment working is not the same thing as the equipment changing anyone's behaviour, and a great many corporate information systems meet exactly this same fate, technically functional, organisationally ignored.

The business example with numbers is the customer relationship management system, the CRM, which we will walk through as a full story shortly, but the principle deserves stating plainly here first. A company can spend a genuinely large sum implementing a CRM system, train every salesperson to log every call, watch the dashboards populate with impressively detailed charts showing pipeline stages, conversion rates, and customer histories, and still extract zero value from the entire investment if not one single sales decision, who to call first, which deal to prioritise, which account is at risk of churning, actually changes because of what the dashboard shows. The system worked. The decisions did not change. And a system is genuinely worth exactly the value of the decisions it changes, nothing more, regardless of how much it cost to build or how impressive it looks in a boardroom demonstration.

When do you reach for this discipline. At every single stage of every information systems project you are ever involved in evaluating, funding, or sponsoring, and especially at the two moments organisations most often skip it, at the requirements stage, where the discipline is to genuinely involve the people who will use the system daily rather than consulting them briefly and moving on, and at the post launch stage, where the discipline is to measure adoption and decision change months after go live, not merely whether the launch date was hit.

So remember. Most large information systems projects fail not because the technology does not work, but because requirements were frozen too early, deployment happened all at once instead of in phases, and success was measured at the moment of launch rather than the moment, much later, that decisions on the ground actually started changing. A system, however expensive and however impressive its dashboards, is worth exactly the decisions it changes, and a dashboard nobody acts on is nothing more than a screensaver with a licence fee attached.

Let us now sit inside two fuller stories, because both of these ideas, the danger of a free product hiding a different business model, and the danger of a system that launches successfully but changes nothing, are easiest to hold onto once you have watched them play out completely.

The security company story begins in an industry that had, for years, felt almost boringly stable. Antivirus software companies sold annual subscriptions, users paid, largely without complaint, because the alternative, an infected computer, felt expensive and frightening enough to make the subscription fee feel entirely reasonable. Several large, profitable companies had built their entire business around this single, dependable transaction, repeated every year, across tens of millions of paying households and businesses. Then a company entered the market with a proposition that seemed, on the surface, almost suicidal from a business perspective, full antivirus protection, genuinely effective, offered completely free, with no subscription, no trial period that expired into a paywall, simply free, indefinitely, to anyone who wanted it. The established players watched this happen and, for a while, assumed the obvious thing, that this was an unsustainable customer acquisition tactic, a company burning venture capital to buy users before eventually introducing fees of its own once it had built a large enough base, the same playbook plenty of software companies had run before. They waited for the fees to arrive. The fees never arrived. What arrived instead was a slow, then rapid, collapse in the subscription revenue of the entire industry, as millions of users, quite reasonably, switched to the free alternative, since it protected their computers just as effectively as the software they had been paying for. Several long standing, profitable antivirus companies found their core revenue line shrinking year over year, with no obvious way to compete against a rival charging nothing at all. The company offering the free product, meanwhile, was not struggling. It had built something the subscription model never gave it, direct, trusted, default presence on tens of millions of machines, software that users had knowingly installed and continued to run, granting it a level of access and attention that money could not easily buy. It turned that installed base into a browser product, defaulted into daily use by millions of the same users who had installed the free antivirus, and it built relationships with app developers eager to reach that same enormous, engaged user base through app distribution deals bundled alongside the security software. None of this new revenue had anything to do with the price of antivirus protection itself, which is exactly the point, and exactly why the incumbent antivirus companies, no matter how sharply they eventually cut their own prices in response, could never fully close the gap, because they were still fighting a price war for a product whose value the market leader had already decided to give away for free, permanently, funded by a business model the incumbents had no presence in at all.

The second story belongs to a mid sized business to business company that decided, after years of salespeople tracking their pipelines in scattered spreadsheets and personal notebooks, to implement a proper customer relationship management system, the kind with dashboards, automated reminders, pipeline visualisations, and detailed customer interaction histories that every modern sales organisation is expected to have. The rollout, on paper, went well. The system launched on schedule. Every salesperson received training. The dashboards, once populated with a few months of data, looked genuinely impressive in the quarterly leadership review, colour coded pipeline stages, conversion funnels, customer engagement scores updating in something close to real time. Leadership treated the project as a clear success, and in the narrow sense of the system existing, working correctly, and being technically adopted in the sense of data entry, it was. But roughly a year in, a newly hired sales operations analyst asked a question nobody else had thought to ask directly, not whether people were using the system, but whether anyone's actual behaviour had changed because of what the system showed them. She pulled records from before the CRM existed and compared them against records from the year after, looking specifically at which accounts salespeople prioritised calling first each week, and found something uncomfortable, the pattern was almost identical. Salespeople were dutifully logging their calls into the new system after they made them, exactly as trained, but they were still deciding who to call, in what order, and which deals to chase hardest, using the same gut instinct and habitual relationships they had always used, entirely independent of what the dashboard's engagement scores or pipeline risk flags actually said. The system had become an elaborate record keeping exercise layered on top of decisions that were being made exactly as they always had been. It had cost a genuinely large sum to implement, consumed months of every salesperson's training time, and it was, by the honest measure this course insists on, worth close to nothing, because not a single decision on the ground had actually changed. The company, to its credit, did not simply abandon the system once this became clear. It rebuilt the rollout around a much narrower, sharper question, forcing the dashboard's risk flag for accounts likely to churn to become the mandatory first stop of every Monday morning sales meeting, not a background chart anyone could ignore, but the literal first item discussed, with each flagged account requiring an explicit decision recorded on the spot. Only once the system's output was wired directly into an actual weekly decision, rather than sitting passively available for anyone who happened to look, did the churn rate on flagged accounts begin to genuinely improve, a full eighteen months after the original, technically successful, organisationally invisible launch.

Now the traps, because managing information systems has a particular set of misconceptions that catch smart, well intentioned people surprisingly often.

The first trap is assuming every technology decision is really a technology decision, when very often it is a business model decision wearing technical language, exactly as the security company story demonstrates, where the real battle was never about which antivirus engine detected more viruses.

The second trap is treating platform and pipeline as a permanent identity rather than a strategic choice, and missing when a pipeline competitor is quietly building platform characteristics into its business, or when a platform is drifting into owning inventory it should be renting instead.

The third trap is reaching for the newest, most discussed technology in the room simply because it is the newest, most discussed technology in the room, skipping the right tool test entirely, and building genuine organisational complexity to solve a trust problem that a plain database, or plain trust between people who already trust each other, would have solved for a fraction of the cost.

The fourth trap, and perhaps the most expensive one across real corporate history, is declaring an information systems project a success at the moment it launches, when the only honest measure of success arrives much later, and requires someone to go back and check, deliberately and specifically, whether real decisions changed.

And the fifth trap is building a dashboard, however sophisticated, and stopping there, mistaking the existence of information for the presence of value, when value only appears the moment a specific person makes a specific decision differently because of what that dashboard told them.

Let us close with the revision sprint. Network effects mean a product's value can come almost entirely from the size of the network around it rather than from the product itself, which is why network businesses spend aggressively and seemingly irrationally to win early users, they are manufacturing the asset the business depends on. Platform businesses own the connection between two sides of a market rather than the inventory being exchanged, which lets them scale faster than pipeline businesses, but only after they solve the chicken and egg problem, typically by subsidising whichever side of the market is more price sensitive. When a competitor gives away your core product for free, resist assuming incompetence or temporary tactics, and ask instead what they gain simply from having that product installed and trusted, because you may be fighting a different business model rather than a price war. The right tool test asks one question before adopting any new technology, does a trustworthy central keeper of records genuinely not exist here, and if one does, the cheap, boring database usually wins. And most large information systems projects fail for the same three reasons, requirements frozen too early, deployment happening all at once instead of gradually, and success measured at launch rather than at the much later moment when decisions on the ground either change or quietly do not.

Your one sentence for managing information systems is this. Networks compound because value comes from other people's presence, platforms own connections rather than assets, free products very often signal a different business model hunting you, every new technology must justify itself against a cheaper alternative, and every system, however impressive its launch, is worth exactly the decisions it eventually changes.
