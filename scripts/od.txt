The town hall was full, every seat taken, a few people standing at the back near the coffee urn nobody had touched. The head of operations was six months into the new system rollout, and today was the day she stood up to declare the transformation a success. The numbers on the screen behind her were genuinely good. Processing time down. Error rate down. Two pilot sites running smoothly for eleven straight weeks. She thanked the project team by name, thanked the early adopters who had championed the new workflow when nobody else believed in it, and closed with a line that got a real round of applause: we did it, and now we can all get back to normal.

Everyone clapped. Everyone meant it. And that sentence, we can all get back to normal, was the exact moment the whole transformation began quietly dying, though nobody in that room could have told you that at the time.

Eight months later, a junior analyst pulled the usage data almost by accident, checking something unrelated, and found that four of the six rolled out sites had drifted back to the old workflow almost entirely. Not through rebellion. Not through any decision anyone had made on purpose. The project team that used to walk the floor answering questions had been reassigned to the next priority the week after that town hall. The temporary incentive for using the new system had quietly expired and nobody renewed it. The old workflow's shortcuts, still technically available in the system because nobody had switched them off, were simply easier when nobody was watching which button you clicked. Within eight months, the transformation that had been celebrated with applause had evaporated, not with a bang, not with a single dramatic reversal, but through a hundred small unwatched choices, each one perfectly reasonable on its own, each one a tiny step back toward the way things had always been done.

This is, almost beat for beat, the most common way organisational change actually dies. Not through a big visible failure. Through a premature victory lap, followed by exactly nobody left standing guard over the new way of doing things while the old gravity, patient and never actually removed, pulls everything quietly back down.

Hello, and welcome to the Decision Notebook listening library. Today, organisational change and culture. This is the episode about why change is announced constantly and completed rarely. We will build a working model of how change actually moves through three acts, look underneath it at the deeper physics of why anything resists changing at all, learn to read resistance itself as useful information rather than an obstacle to crush, and finish inside the layer of culture that quietly outlasts every visible policy change stacked on top of it.

If there is one thread running underneath today's entire episode, it is this. Change does not fail because people are stubborn or lazy or resistant to progress for its own sake. Change fails because the forces holding the old way in place are never actually removed, only temporarily overpowered, and the moment the overpowering stops, and it always eventually stops, whatever was underneath reasserts itself.

Think about what happens when you hold a spring compressed and then let go. The spring does not stay compressed because you wanted it to, or because compressing it once was hard work, or because you gave a speech about the virtues of staying compressed. It stays compressed only as long as something is actively holding it there. Let go, and physics does not care how good your intentions were. Organisational change has exactly this property, and almost every change programme that quietly reverts, the diet that ends in the same weight six months later, the new process that slides back into the old one, the reorganisation that has functionally undone itself within two years, is a story of the compressing force being removed before anything was actually installed to hold the new shape in place on its own.

This is why the language we use around change matters more than it seems to. Words like rollout, and launch, and implementation, all quietly imply an ending, a finish line you cross and then stop running. But if change behaves like a compressed spring, there is no finish line in that sense. There is only the point where you stop applying force and start finding out whether something else has taken over the holding. Keep that distinction in your head through everything that follows, because every framework today is really just a more precise description of either how to compress the spring in the first place, or how to build something that holds it there once you let go.

Let us build the backbone framework first, because everything else today sits on top of it.

Kotter's eight step model describes change as moving through three acts, and it is worth understanding the acts before memorising the steps, because the acts explain why the steps are ordered the way they are. Act one is unfreezing the current state. This is where you build real urgency, not manufactured panic but a genuine, widely shared sense that standing still is dangerous, assemble a coalition of people with actual power and credibility rather than just good intentions, and form and communicate a vision clearly enough that people two levels removed from the leadership team could repeat it in a single sentence. Act two is the actual moving. You clear away the structural obstacles blocking the new behaviour, you generate visible short term wins early enough that belief has something real to point at, and you keep pressing rather than easing off the moment the first win arrives. Act three is refreezing. You anchor the new way of doing things into the systems that will keep running long after the change programme itself has been disbanded, the incentives, the promotion criteria, the onboarding material, the actual muscle memory of how work gets done here.

In plain words, this is exactly the ice cube analogy the model's name suggests. You cannot reshape an ice cube while it is frozen solid, you have to melt it first, unfreezing, reshape it while it is fluid, moving, and then let it set into the new shape, refreezing, so it holds without anyone standing there with a mould in their hands forever. Skip the first stage and people simply do not believe change is necessary, so they wait it out. Skip the last stage and you get exactly the town hall story from the top of this episode, a shape that looked right for a moment and melted straight back once the pressure came off.

An everyday example: think about trying to get a household to actually recycle properly, going past the point of merely agreeing in principle that recycling matters. You need urgency first, some real reason this matters to this specific household, not a vague global one. You need a coalition, at least one other person in the house who genuinely believes in it and will not let it slide the first busy week. You need a vision simple enough that a ten year old could repeat it, everything wet in one bin, everything dry in the other. You need quick wins, the neighbour noticing and complimenting the new bins within the first fortnight. And critically, you need refreezing, the new bins have to become the only bins in the kitchen, because if the old single bin is still sitting there under the sink, available, familiar, and slightly less effort, the household will drift back to it within a month the moment anyone stops paying close attention.

The business version, and the specific insight worth the price of admission today, is where most transformations actually die. It is not step one, urgency, and it is not the difficult middle steps either. The evidence points overwhelmingly to step seven, sustaining acceleration, the step most leadership teams treat as basically finished business the moment the first genuine wins appear. Early results land, the leadership team celebrates publicly, attention and resourcing quietly redirect to the next priority on the list, and eighteen months later, someone doing a routine audit discovers the organisation has silently reverted most of the way back to where it started. It dies of success, which is precisely why it is so hard to catch in the moment, because celebrating an early win feels like exactly the right thing to do, and it is, right up until the celebration is mistaken for the finish line rather than the halfway point.

Use this framework specifically whenever you are diagnosing why a change effort stalled, or whenever you are about to declare victory on one yourself. Walk the eight steps in order and ask honestly which one was actually skipped or cut short, rather than assuming the whole model failed. Most of the time the diagnosis is embarrassingly specific: someone skipped urgency and wondered why nobody engaged, or someone nailed everything through the early wins and then quietly stopped applying pressure the moment the applause started.

So remember: change moves through unfreezing, moving, and refreezing, most failures cluster at the step where early wins get mistaken for the finish line, and a short term win is fuel for the harder work still ahead of you, never the destination itself.

Underneath Kotter's eight steps sits an older, more fundamental idea from Kurt Lewin, and once you see it, you cannot stop noticing it everywhere, including well outside the office.

The force field model says any stable current state, any equilibrium, is not stability in the sense of nothing pushing on it. It is a balance between two opposing sets of forces, driving forces pushing toward change, and restraining forces pushing back to keep things exactly as they are. The situation looks calm on the surface only because the two sets of forces happen to be roughly equal, not because either set is weak or absent.

And here is the genuinely counterintuitive part, the piece that surprises people even after they have heard the model explained once already. The instinctive move, when you want change, is to push harder, add more driving force, more pressure, more urgency, more incentive. But Lewin's model, and decades of evidence behind it, suggest the more reliable lever is usually the opposite one: identify and remove a restraining force instead. The reason is not mystical, it is almost mechanical. Adding driving force raises the total tension in the whole system, and rising tension tends to also strengthen resistance, because people under more pressure tend to dig in rather than relax, which means you can spend enormous energy pushing and net out with barely any actual movement, all of it absorbed by resistance that grew in exact proportion to your push. Removing a restraining force, by contrast, lowers total tension in the system, and the existing driving forces, the ones already there, now have room to move things on their own, without needing to be strengthened at all.

An everyday example that lands immediately: getting a child to spend less time watching television. The instinctive move is to add driving force, more nagging, more rules, more consequences threatened with increasing volume. It usually produces exactly what Lewin's model predicts, more resistance, meeting the rising pressure with rising defiance, and a household that is now louder and no closer to the actual goal. The more effective route, and any parent who has tried both will tell you this from lived experience rather than theory, is removing a restraining force instead, moving the television out of the child's bedroom and into a shared space, for instance, which changes nothing about the rules or the nagging and everything about the actual behaviour, because you removed the thing that was making the old behaviour easy rather than trying to out shout it.

The business version shows up constantly in change management, and it explains a pattern many managers have lived through without naming it. A leadership team wants faster decision making. The instinctive move is to add driving force, exhortations to move faster, deadlines, dashboards tracking speed, sometimes even financial incentives tied to velocity. And it frequently produces very little actual change, because the real restraining force was never a lack of motivation to move fast, it was a genuine structural obstacle, an approval process running through four separate committees that nobody had the authority to bypass. Remove that one restraining force, collapse the four committees into one, and decision speed improves immediately, without a single motivational push added anywhere, because the driving forces that already wanted faster decisions, and there were plenty of them, finally had room to actually operate.

Use this framework whenever you are staring at a stuck situation and your instinct is simply to push harder. Before adding more pressure, force yourself to map the restraining side of the field first, honestly and completely, and ask which of those restraining forces could actually be removed, rather than which driving force could be added on top of an already tense system.

So remember: every stable state is a balance of opposing forces, not an absence of pressure, and removing what is holding things back usually beats adding more push, because pushing harder raises tension across the whole system while removing a barrier lowers it.

Pause here and try this yourself. Think of one change you or your team have been trying, and mostly failing, to make stick for a while now, something you have pushed on more than once without much lasting movement. Before reading on, try mapping it honestly as a force field. List every driving force you can think of, everything already pushing toward this change. Then list every restraining force, everything actively holding the current state in place. Here is what usually happens when people do this properly instead of skipping it. Most people discover they can name the driving forces easily, because those are the reasons they wanted the change in the first place and they have said them out loud many times. The restraining forces are much harder to name, because naming them honestly often means naming an inconvenient truth, a broken incentive, a process nobody wants to admit is the real obstacle, a person whose interests are genuinely served by things staying exactly as they are. If your list of restraining forces came out thin or vague, that is worth noticing, because a restraining force you cannot name is one you also cannot remove, and you will likely keep pushing on the driving side, wondering why so little moves.

Our third framework reframes something almost every manager privately dreads: the moment someone on the team pushes back against a change.

Kotter and Schlesinger, building directly on this same tradition, identified four distinct root causes of resistance, and their most valuable insight is that each cause needs a completely different remedy, so treating all resistance as the same problem with the same fix, usually more persuasion or more pressure, wastes effort at best and creates a genuine enemy at worst.

The first cause is self interest. Someone resists because the change genuinely, concretely costs them something, status, workload, a comfortable routine, sometimes even their actual job. The honest remedy here is negotiation, openly acknowledging the real cost and working out what, if anything, can offset it, rather than pretending the cost does not exist.

The second cause is misunderstanding, often paired with simple low trust in whoever is delivering the message. People resist because they do not actually understand what is changing or why, or because they do not trust the source enough to take the explanation at face value. The remedy here is communication, repeated, clear, and delivered by someone the audience actually trusts, not a single memo sent once and considered complete.

The third cause is low tolerance for change itself, a genuine, sometimes even physiological discomfort with uncertainty and disruption that has little to do with the specific change on the table. The remedy is support, training, transition time, and patience, giving people room to build confidence in the new way before expecting fluency.

The fourth cause is the one leaders find hardest to accept, and it is arguably the most important one in the whole model: different assessment. The person resisting has looked at the same situation and genuinely, honestly, come to a different conclusion about whether this is the right change, or the right way to make it. And here is the humbling possibility Kotter and Schlesinger insist you sit with: the resister might simply be right, or at least partly right, holding a piece of information or experience the change's designers missed. The remedy here is participation, genuinely involving that person in shaping the plan, not as a box ticking gesture but as real input that could actually change the plan. This produces one of two good outcomes, and both are wins. Either the resister's input improves the plan in a way that would not have happened otherwise, or the act of genuine participation converts the resister into a supporter, because people rarely fight plans they helped build.

An everyday example: think about a family deciding to move to a new city. One family member resists loudly. If the resistance is really about losing proximity to old friends, that is self interest, and the honest remedy is acknowledging the real loss and finding ways to soften it, regular visits planned in advance, for instance. If the resistance is really about not understanding why the move is even necessary, that is misunderstanding, and the remedy is a proper, patient conversation laying out the actual reasons. But if the resistance is because that family member has lived through a bad move before and has specific, concrete concerns about the new city's schools that nobody else in the family has actually researched, that is different assessment, and dismissing it as mere stubbornness would be a mistake, because the honest move is to sit down together and actually look at the schools.

The business version is the senior engineer who pushes back hard against a new system architecture the leadership team has already decided to adopt. It is tempting, and common, to read this as simple resistance to change, and to respond by applying pressure, reminding her the decision has already been made. But suppose her resistance is rooted in a failure mode she has personally seen before, in a previous system, one the current design has not accounted for. That is different assessment, not stubbornness, and the correct remedy is participation: bring her formally into the design process, let her stress test the plan against the exact failure she is worried about. Either she finds the plan genuinely handles it, and her concern is resolved along with everyone else's confidence in the plan, or she finds a real gap, and the organisation just avoided a costly failure it would otherwise have walked straight into.

Use this framework the moment you feel yourself irritated by someone resisting a change you believe in. That irritation is a signal to diagnose, not to push harder. Ask which of the four causes is actually operating before choosing a remedy, because negotiating with someone who is confused, or lecturing someone who is right, both waste the same afternoon for entirely different reasons.

So remember: resistance is information, not obstruction, it has four different roots, self interest, misunderstanding, low tolerance, and different assessment, and each one has its own remedy, with participation as the answer whenever the resister might simply know something you do not.

Pause here and try this yourself. Think of the last time someone resisted a decision you had already made, at work or at home. Before reading on, try honestly sorting that resistance into one of the four causes, rather than defaulting to the label most people reach for automatically, which is simply that the person did not like change. Here is the pattern worth noticing once you have done this a few times. People tend to default to labelling almost all resistance as either self interest or low tolerance, because those two are the easiest to dismiss without much self examination. Different assessment is the category people skip most often, precisely because taking it seriously means entertaining the possibility that the resister has a point, which is uncomfortable. If, looking back honestly, the resistance you are thinking of might actually have been different assessment, ask yourself what would have happened had you brought that person in to shape the plan rather than simply overruling them.

Our final framework for today explains why some organisational change lands quickly and completely, while other change of exactly the same apparent size seems to bounce off the organisation entirely, leaving barely a mark six months later.

Edgar Schein's model describes culture as sitting in three distinct layers, and the depth of each layer determines how easily it can actually be changed. The top layer is artifacts, everything visible on the surface, the office layout, the dress code, the language people use in meetings, the posters on the wall. Artifacts are easy to see and, relatively speaking, easy to change, you can repaint a wall or issue a new dress code by Monday. The middle layer is espoused values, the things people in the organisation will tell you they believe, printed on the website, recited in the induction session, genuinely meant by most people who say them out loud. The bottom layer, far deeper and almost invisible even to the people living inside it, is basic assumptions, the things everyone in the organisation simply takes for granted, so thoroughly unquestioned that nobody actually says them out loud, because saying them out loud would feel as strange as announcing that the sky is blue.

Most change programmes, even well designed ones, operate almost entirely on the top two layers. They change artifacts, a new open plan office, a new logo, and they change espoused values, a new set of stated principles announced at an all hands meeting. And this is precisely why so many well intentioned change programmes fail to actually change anything real: they never touch the third layer, because the third layer was never articulated in the first place, and it is extremely difficult to deliberately address an assumption nobody has ever put into words.

An everyday example: think of two households that both say, and genuinely believe, that they value open communication, an espoused value both families would state identically if you asked them directly. But in one household, the deep, unspoken assumption is that conflict is dangerous and should be avoided at almost any cost, so open communication in practice means only ever discussing pleasant things. In the other household, the unspoken assumption is that conflict, handled respectfully, is actually a normal and healthy part of being close to someone, so open communication in practice includes real disagreement. Both households would give you the same answer on a survey. Their actual behaviour, shaped by two entirely different basic assumptions neither family has ever consciously named, looks nothing alike.

The business version shows up most dramatically in mergers between two individually healthy companies, and it is one of the more reliable predictors of merger friction. Both companies might have excellent, genuinely admirable espoused values, printed proudly in nearly identical language on both sets of office walls, teamwork, integrity, customer focus, the usual list. And yet the merged organisation experiences chronic, hard to diagnose friction for months or years, meetings that somehow always feel slightly adversarial, decisions that take far longer than either company took on its own, a persistent low grade sense that something is simply not working even though nobody can point to a specific broken process. Often, underneath all of that, sits a genuine collision at the level of basic assumptions that predates the merger entirely and was never written down anywhere, one company having built its entire rhythm around an unspoken assumption that decisions move fast and mistakes get corrected later, the other having built its rhythm around an unspoken assumption that decisions move slowly and carefully because mistakes are expensive and hard to reverse. Neither assumption is wrong on its own. Colliding, unnamed, and unaddressed, they produce exactly the chronic low grade friction that so many merger integration teams spend months trying to fix at the artifact and espoused value layers, new shared logos, new joint value statements, without ever touching the actual source, because the actual source was never named.

Use this framework whenever a change effort has technically been implemented, new policies exist, new values have been announced, and yet something still feels stuck, unconvincing, or quietly resisted in a way nobody can quite put their finger on. That feeling is usually a signal that the real obstacle is sitting at the third layer, and the honest next step is not another poster or another values workshop, it is the slower, harder work of actually surfacing what people assume without saying, often through structured conversation rather than a survey, because assumptions this deep rarely show up in a form with tick boxes.

So remember: culture lives in three layers, visible artifacts, stated values, and unspoken basic assumptions, and most change programmes only ever touch the first two, which is exactly why so many of them fail to produce change that actually holds.

Now let us slow all the way down and spend real time inside two full stories, because these frameworks only prove their worth once you watch them play out completely, start to finish, including the parts that go wrong.

The first story returns to the town hall from the very beginning of this episode, and it is worth walking through in full, because almost every detail in it maps directly onto Kotter's model. The rollout had, genuinely, done the early steps well. There had been real urgency, a credible, well documented case that the old system was going to become a serious liability within eighteen months regardless of what anyone decided. There had been a real coalition, the operations head standing alongside two other senior leaders who had put their own credibility behind the project publicly. There had been a clear vision, simple enough that most staff could repeat the core idea correctly when asked. There had been genuine short term wins, the two pilot sites running cleanly for eleven straight weeks were not manufactured numbers, they were real, and the organisation was right to feel encouraged by them.

What never happened, and this is the entire story compressed into a single missing step, was refreezing. Nobody redesigned the incentive structure to reward use of the new system over the old one on an ongoing basis, past the initial pilot period. Nobody removed the old workflow's shortcuts from the system entirely, so the path of least resistance back to the familiar routine remained fully available the moment attention drifted elsewhere. Nobody kept a small, permanent team walking the floor answering questions after the celebratory town hall, because that team had been reassigned to the next priority the following week, a perfectly sensible individual decision that, in aggregate, amounted to letting go of the compressed spring. The town hall itself, the applause, the sincere thanks to the early adopters, none of that was the mistake. The mistake was mistaking that moment for the end of the work rather than the exact point where a different, quieter, less applause worthy kind of work needed to begin, the work of making the new shape hold on its own, without anyone standing there compressing it by hand.

When the junior analyst's data surfaced the reversion eight months later, the organisation did have one genuine advantage most do not: it caught the drift while it was still partial, four of six sites rather than all of them, and the fix that followed was instructive precisely because it finally addressed the missing layer. Leadership did not simply repeat the town hall, another round of urgency and vision, which had never been the missing ingredient. They rewrote the incentive structure so that using the new workflow was actually, permanently, tied to performance metrics staff cared about. They removed the old shortcuts from the system entirely, rather than merely discouraging them. And they built the new workflow's steps directly into the onboarding material for every new hire going forward, so the new way of working stopped being something people had chosen once during a rollout and became, instead, simply how things were done here, the same way nobody consciously decides each morning which side of the road to drive on. That last move is refreezing in its purest form, and it is the piece almost every failed change programme skipped, right at the moment it felt most finished.

The second story is a composite of the kind every experienced change consultant will tell you they have seen more than once, a mid sized manufacturing company attempting to move from a traditional, hierarchical approval process to a faster, more decentralised decision structure. The driving forces were real and numerous: competitors were moving faster, customers were increasingly frustrated by slow turnaround, and a genuine cohort of younger managers inside the company wanted more autonomy and had been vocal about it for over a year. Leadership, reading the situation correctly as one with strong driving forces already present, made the instinctive move, launching an ambitious change programme with new decision rights formally granted on paper to middle managers, backed by an energetic communication campaign.

Six months in, decision speed had barely moved, and leadership was genuinely puzzled, because on paper the change had been made cleanly and communicated clearly. What the change programme had never done was seriously map the restraining forces already present in the system, because the entire design effort had gone into strengthening the driving side, more authority, more encouragement, more communication, without ever asking what had been holding decisions slow in the first place. It turned out, once someone finally sat down with the middle managers directly rather than surveying them at a distance, that the real restraining force was not a lack of formal authority at all, it was an unwritten, widely understood expectation that any decision which later went wrong would be personally, individually blamed on whichever manager had made the call, regardless of how reasonable the decision looked at the time it was made. Formal authority to decide faster meant nothing against a deeply embedded fear of being individually blamed for a fast decision that later proved wrong, and every manager, quietly and rationally, kept routing decisions upward for cover, new decision rights sitting unused on paper exactly as before.

This is a textbook instance of what Kotter and Schlesinger's resistance model would classify, at first glance, as low tolerance for change, or perhaps self interest, protecting one's own position. It looked, from a distance, like ordinary reluctance. But it was closer to different assessment, arrived at collectively rather than by a single named individual: the managers, correctly, assessed that the real risk of the new system had never actually been addressed by the leadership announcing it, and factored that unaddressed risk into their behaviour long before anyone in leadership admitted it existed. The eventual fix, once diagnosed correctly, was not another motivational push for speed. It was a new, explicit policy protecting managers who made reasonable decisions in good faith from individual blame when outcomes later went wrong, provided the decision process itself had been sound. That single restraining force, once actually removed rather than argued around, did more for decision speed within a single quarter than six months of authority grants and communication campaigns combined, because the driving forces pushing toward faster decisions had been genuinely strong from the very beginning. They had simply never had room to operate.

Before we close, a short pass through the ways today's ideas get misapplied, because misapplying a good framework is often worse than not knowing it at all.

The first trap is treating Kotter's eight steps as a rigid checklist to be completed once, in strict sequence, and then filed away as done. The steps describe a logic, not a project plan with a finish date, and step seven and eight in particular are not one time events, they describe an ongoing posture that has to continue well past the point where the change programme officially ends and becomes, ideally, invisible ordinary practice.

The second trap, with the force field model, is assuming removing restraining forces is always gentler or always easier than adding driving force. It is neither, necessarily. Some restraining forces are deeply structural, tied to real incentives or real fears, and removing them can be harder and slower than any motivational campaign. The insight is not that removing barriers is easy, it is that removing barriers tends to be more effective per unit of effort than adding pressure to an already tense system, which is a different and more precise claim.

The third trap, with resistance, is using the different assessment category as a blanket excuse to delay every change indefinitely under the guise of listening. Genuine participation has a natural endpoint, a decision eventually has to be made, and treating every objection as potentially valid forever is its own kind of paralysis, just as damaging as ignoring objections outright.

And the final trap, with Schein's three layers, is assuming basic assumptions can be changed quickly through the right kind of workshop or the right kind of consultant. They generally cannot, not on the timeline most change programmes are funded and staffed for. Naming a basic assumption honestly is genuinely useful and often the necessary first step, but naming it is not the same as having changed it, and organisations that treat the naming as the finish line often find themselves disappointed a year later for reasons that look, uncomfortably, exactly like today's opening story.

Let us bring today home with a short revision sprint.

Change moves through three acts, unfreezing, moving, and refreezing, and the evidence points to step seven, sustaining acceleration, as where most transformations actually die, usually from a premature declaration of victory rather than active opposition. Underneath that sits Lewin's force field, every stable state a balance of driving and restraining forces, and removing what is holding things back usually beats pushing harder on what is already pushing forward, because added pressure raises tension across the whole system while removed barriers lower it. Resistance itself is information with four distinct roots, self interest, misunderstanding, low tolerance, and different assessment, each needing its own remedy, and participation as the answer whenever the resister might genuinely know something the plan's designers missed. And underneath all of that sits culture in three layers, artifacts, espoused values, and basic assumptions, with most change programmes only ever reaching the first two, which is exactly why so much visible change fails to become real, lasting change.

Your one sentence for organizational dynamics: unfreeze, move, and actually refreeze, remove barriers before adding pressure, treat every resister as a possible source of information rather than an obstacle, and never mistake an early win, or a stated value, for the deeper work still required to make it last.
