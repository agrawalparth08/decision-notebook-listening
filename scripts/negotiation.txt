A film director sits across the table from Betsy, a producer, negotiating salary for his next picture. Betsy opens low, matching his last film's fee. He pushes back, no number yet, just his track record. She moves up a little. He jumps, in one breath, to a number more than three times hers, citing profits that ran ten to a hundred times past investments. She counters with a long, careful speech about risk. He mentions two other production houses circling him, sets a deadline, and says meet me there or we part friends below a certain line. She holds firm at her ceiling. He accepts it. On paper it looks like a win, he did move her number up substantially from where she opened. But when the class ran the numbers afterward, on the scoring rubric built into the exercise, he had closed at roughly half of the value that was actually sitting on the table, because he had never once, in four rounds of back and forth, asked Betsy what she actually needed the deal to look like. He negotiated a single number, salary, when the real contract could have carried profit share, sequel rights, creative control, and a dozen other terms that would have cost the producer very little and been worth a great deal to him. He won the argument and lost most of the value.

This is the course that turns out to be less about tactics at the table and more about the preparation that happens before anyone sits down. We are going to build your understanding of where negotiating power actually comes from, walk through the zone where a deal is even possible and what happens when that zone does not exist, spend real time on the difference between what someone demands and what they actually need, and then watch three classroom negotiations, a salary fight, a coffee contract, and a cartoon licensing deal, to see these ideas either used well or left on the table. By the end you should be able to walk into your next negotiation, whatever it is, salary, a vendor contract, a family property dispute, with an actual plan rather than just an opening number and your nerves.

One more thing worth saying before we build the toolkit properly. Most people prepare for a negotiation by rehearsing what they will say. The professor's own emphasis runs the other way, toward what you should find out before you say anything at all. Three things belong on paper before you walk into any negotiation that matters. Your own BATNA, written down in actual numbers, not a vague sense that you have options. Your honest best guess at the other side's underlying interests, not their stated position. And at least one additional issue you could bring to the table beyond the headline number everyone is expecting to argue about. Negotiators who skip this preparation are not lacking in skill at the table. They are lacking in information before they ever sit down, and no amount of table skill fully compensates for walking in blind.

Let us start with the one idea that changes more outcomes than any tactic taught in this course.

Negotiating power does not come from confidence. It does not come from aggression, or from a good speech, or from being the more senior person in the room. It comes from your alternatives. Your BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement, is simply what you will do if the deal in front of you falls apart. A candidate holding two job offers walks into a salary conversation relaxed and genuinely willing to say no. A candidate holding zero offers agrees to almost anything, because the alternative to agreeing is nothing at all, and nothing is usually worse than a bad deal. This gives you the single highest value insight in the entire course, and it is worth saying plainly: the best negotiation move most people ever make happens outside the negotiation, days or weeks before it starts, when they go and quietly build themselves another option. One more credible alternative moves your outcome further than any clever line you could say across the table. Tactics redistribute value that already exists inside a narrow range. Your alternatives are what set the size of that range in the first place, before a single word is exchanged.

This is also why so much negotiation training focused only on tactics, how to phrase your opening line, how to hold a pause, quietly misses the point. A brilliant tactic used from a weak alternative still produces a weak outcome, because the other side can sense, correctly, that you have nowhere else to go. And a mediocre tactic used from a strong alternative still produces a decent outcome, because the other side knows, correctly, that pushing you too far means losing the deal entirely. So before we get to any of the specific tools in this episode, hold this thread through all of them: every framework we are about to cover works by helping you either strengthen your own alternatives, understand the other side's alternatives, or find value that neither side had to trade against their alternatives at all. Preparation is not the boring part of negotiation. It is the negotiation. What happens at the table is mostly the execution of decisions that were already made, correctly or badly, before you sat down.

Let us make the BATNA idea precise, because most people who use the word have only half understood it. Your BATNA sets your reservation point, the true walk away number below which, or above which, you will simply say no and leave. Notice the reservation point is not your target, and it is not your opening ask. It is the floor, or the ceiling, underneath your negotiating range, and it exists whether or not you have bothered to write it down. The discipline the course insists on is writing it down anyway, in rupees, on paper, before you walk into the room, because a reservation point that lives only in your head has a way of quietly drifting during the conversation, especially once the other side starts pushing.

Think of buying a used car. You decide, before you visit the seller, that you will not pay more than four lakh rupees for this particular model, because at four lakh twenty you would rather keep looking. That number is your reservation point. If you walk in without deciding it in advance, the seller's confident opening number becomes, without your noticing, the anchor your own sense of a fair price drifts toward, and you can easily talk yourself into four lakh eighty by the end of a pleasant conversation, congratulating yourself on a good negotiation the whole way home.

In business, a company negotiating a two year office lease knows its BATNA is a co working space at seventy thousand rupees a month, available immediately, no negotiation required. That number is not a bluff or a threat to be waved at the landlord. It is simply true, and it is what lets the company's representative sit calmly through a landlord's opening ask of one lakh ten thousand a month, because everyone in the room can tell the difference between someone who is posturing and someone who genuinely has somewhere else to go.

So remember: your BATNA is your real fallback, your reservation point is the walk away line it produces, and the entire quality of your negotiating position depends on how strong that fallback actually is, not on how well you argue once you are already sitting at the table.

Now put two parties' reservation points side by side and you get the ZOPA, the zone of possible agreement, the overlap between what the seller will accept at minimum and what the buyer will pay at maximum. Here is the part people resist accepting, and it is worth saying directly: sometimes that zone does not exist at all. Picture a seller who will not go below ninety, and a buyer who will not go above eighty. There is no number that satisfies both. The honest, correct outcome of that negotiation is a polite handshake and no deal, and here is the reframe the course insists on: walking away from an empty zone is not a failure of negotiation skill, it is success, because any agreement signed inside those facts is simply a mistake wearing two signatures. The skill is not in finding a way to force a deal that should not exist. The skill is in recognising, honestly and early, that it does not exist, so you can stop spending time and goodwill trying to talk one side into accepting a worse outcome than their own alternative.

The more useful skill sits one step earlier, though, and it is this: before declaring the zone empty, ask whether the structure of the deal can change. A seller at ninety and a buyer at eighty look stuck on price alone. But add payment timing, add volume, add a second product line, and the zone that looked empty on one dimension can reopen on several. Declare the zone empty only after you have genuinely tried to restructure the deal, not the moment the first two numbers fail to touch.

So remember: the ZOPA is the overlap between two reservation points, it can genuinely be empty, and walking away from an empty zone is the negotiation working correctly, not the negotiation failing, provided you tried restructuring the deal before you called it empty.

Pause here and try this yourself. Think of the last negotiation you walked away from feeling like you had failed. Write down, honestly, what your reservation point actually was, and what you believe theirs was. If the two genuinely did not overlap, you did not fail that negotiation. You simply met a fact, and recognising a fact quickly is itself a skill most people never practise.

Now the idea that turns arguments into solutions, and it is probably the single most useful reframe in the whole course: the difference between a position and an interest. A position is what someone says they want. An interest is why they want it. Positions collide head on, because two people cannot both have all of one orange. Interests very often do not collide at all, because underneath the collision, the two people frequently want different things entirely. The classic teaching image is exactly that, one orange, two people, both insisting on the whole thing. Split it down the middle and both are half satisfied and a little resentful. Ask each person why they want the orange, and you discover one needs the juice for breakfast and the other needs the peel to bake a cake. Give each the entire part they actually need, and both walk away completely satisfied, from what looked, five minutes earlier, like a fight over a single object with no way to share it.

The question that unlocks this costs four words, and you should use it far more often than you currently do: why do you want that? A vendor insisting on a three year contract is stating a position. Ask why, and you may learn the actual interest is volume certainty for planning next year's capacity, in which case a one year contract with a committed minimum order can satisfy that interest completely, while leaving you free of the two extra years you did not want to commit to. The position said three years. The interest said certainty. Once you are negotiating the interest instead of the position, options appear that were invisible while both sides were simply repeating their numbers at each other.

So remember: positions are what people say, interests are why they say it, and the question why do you want that is worth asking before you counter any demand with a number of your own.

Once interests are genuinely on the table, you can trade across them, and this is logrolling, the engine that turns a negotiation from a fight into something closer to a market. Give generously on what you value less, and take firmly on what you value more, and here is why this works: because two parties almost never value the same issues equally, a single negotiation can produce a result where both sides genuinely believe they won, not because someone was fooled, but because, on each side's own private scoreboard, they actually did win. This is why single issue negotiations, salary and nothing else, price and nothing else, tend to become wars, because there is only one number and someone has to lose the difference. Multi issue negotiations tend to become markets, because there are enough issues on the table that both sides can walk away with the parts that matter most to them. The practical rule that follows is simple: before you sit down, look for a second issue to add to the table, even a small one, because more issues almost always mean more trades, and more trades almost always mean more value for everyone involved, not less.

A useful device for surfacing this in the room itself is offering several equivalent packages at once rather than one number at a time, three offers that are roughly the same value to you but structured completely differently, faster delivery against a lower price, a longer contract against better payment terms, more volume against a discount. Which package the other side reaches for first tells you, without them ever having to say a word out loud, what they actually care about, and that information is worth far more to you than any single counter offer would have been.

So remember: logrolling means giving on what you value less to win on what you value more, always look for a second issue before you sit down, and when you are unsure what the other side actually prioritises, offer several equally good packages and watch which one they pick.

Here is what that trade looks like with real numbers attached, from a job offer negotiation. A candidate cares enormously about base salary, rating it near the top of her own priority list, and barely cares about the sign on bonus, a one time payment that does not compound into her future raises. The employer sits almost exactly the other way round, cost sensitive on base pay because it recurs every year and compounds with every future increment, but genuinely relaxed about a one time sign on bonus, because it happens once and never returns. Push the trade: the candidate gives ground on the sign on bonus and on a long notice period, which she barely values, and gets a materially higher base salary and a senior sounding title in return, both of which the employer can grant at close to no real cost. Neither side lied about what they wanted. Both sides simply told the truth about what they valued least, and traded it for what they valued most. Candidates and employers who negotiate this way routinely report high satisfaction on both sides of the table, while a negotiation confined to base salary alone, argued as a single number back and forth, typically leaves a meaningful share of that same value sitting unclaimed on the table, because nobody ever looked past the one issue everyone assumed was the only issue worth discussing.

Pause here and try this yourself. Take a negotiation you are facing soon, a raise, a vendor renewal, even a family decision about a holiday. List every issue that could plausibly be part of that conversation, not just the headline one. Rank how much you personally care about each. If you can name even one issue you care about less than the other side probably does, you have just found your first logroll, before the conversation has even begun.

Two more tools before we move to the stories. The first is anchoring, and the finding is uncomfortable but well established: the first credible number placed on the table pulls the final outcome strongly in its own direction, far more than most people expect. This means, when you already understand the other side's interests and you have a legitimate number to defend, anchor first. Do not wait politely for them to open. And when they anchor first, at a number that feels disconnected from reality, the correct response is not outrage and it is not silence. It is a calm, explicit re-anchor of your own, grounded in objective criteria, a market rate, a comparable deal, an independent benchmark, so the conversation moves from whose number sounds more confident to whose number is actually defensible.

The second is the contingent contract, and it solves a problem that ordinary bargaining cannot touch: what happens when the two sides simply disagree about what will happen in the future. Rather than arguing over whose forecast is correct, tie the terms to the actual outcome. If your revenue projection is right, you get this. If mine is right, I get that. This is both a resolution to the disagreement and, quietly, a lie detector, because the side that refuses to sign a contract tied to its own forecast is telling you, without meaning to, that it does not actually believe the forecast it has been arguing for so confidently across the table.

Think of a startup founder and an investor disagreeing about next year's revenue. The founder is certain the number will be strong. The investor is doubtful and wants a lower valuation to protect against that doubt. Rather than spending the meeting arguing whose forecast is more believable, they agree on a contingent structure: a lower valuation now, with additional shares released to the founder automatically if revenue clears an agreed target. If the founder is right, both sides know it and the contract already accounts for it. If the investor is right, the lower valuation was the correct call all along. Nobody had to win the argument, because the argument was never really about persuasion, it was about which side was willing to put its own belief on the line.

So remember: anchor first when you know the range and have legitimate grounds, re-anchor with objective criteria when the other side anchors unreasonably, and when you disagree about the future, stop arguing about it and bet on it instead, because a contingent contract settles the argument and exposes the bluff at the same time.

One last practical piece before the stories, because it explains why some negotiators look confident and still lose the room: the shape of your concessions is itself a message, whether you intend it or not. A concession that jumps from ten percent off to forty percent off in a single move tells the other side, unmistakably, that far more room exists behind it, and they will simply keep pushing to find where that room actually ends. A concession that moves in smaller and smaller steps, ten percent, then six, then three, tells the other side, just as clearly, that you are approaching your real limit, even without you ever saying so out loud. Never make the first concession on the issue that matters to you most. Never make two concessions in a row without asking for something back in return. And say the concession out loud when you make it, I am moving here, that is a real move for us, because an unlabelled concession is often not even noticed, and a concession nobody notices buys you no goodwill at all.

So remember: concede in decreasing steps, never twice in a row without getting something back, and always name your concession out loud, because the shape of your giving tells the other side exactly how close to your walk away line you actually are, whether or not you meant to tell them.

Let us now watch these ideas play out in three negotiations run in your own classroom, because the mistakes are more instructive than any textbook description of them.

The first is the one we opened this episode with, the director and the producer, a single issue negotiation over salary. The director opened without a number, citing only his track record. The producer, Betsy, opened low, at two crore, matching his previous film. He countered not with a modest step up but with a jump straight to ten crore, citing profits that had run ten to a hundred times past investments, a bold anchor but one that was never grounded in anything Betsy herself valued. She held her position, offered a compromise at four, and he delivered a long speech about risk reduction rather than asking her a single question about her own constraints. By the third round he had introduced his BATNA, two other production houses, and issued something close to an ultimatum, meet me at ten or we part below eight. Betsy held her ceiling at six crore. He accepted it. The deal closed inside the zone of possible agreement, which the class later worked out ran from roughly three crore to six crore, so it was not a bad deal by the letter of it. But the class scoring showed he had captured perhaps half of the value that was actually available on the table, because Other Terms, profit share, sequel rights, creative control clauses, none of it cost Betsy anything if the film failed and would have cost her very little even if it succeeded, and none of it was ever raised, because he never once asked what would make this project a genuine yes for her side. He played a distributive, single number fight on a stage that could easily have supported an integrative, multi issue conversation, and he never found that out because he never asked.

The second negotiation looked, on its surface, like the same kind of single issue haggle, a hotel food and beverage director negotiating coffee prices with a sales representative from a coffee supplier, but it was carrying a hidden integrative opportunity the whole time. The hotel's real alternative was its incumbent supplier, priced at roughly four dollars seventy a pound. The new supplier's list price opened near eight dollars a pound. On price alone, that is a wide gap to close. But the hotel in this exercise belonged to a university's own hospitality school, and the supplier's marketing team valued that connection, every cup poured there being tasted by future hotel general managers and food and beverage directors, at considerably more than the one or two dollars a pound of margin it might cost to win the account. That hidden lever could have moved the seller's real floor down substantially, from a commodity price near six dollars to something closer to five and a quarter, if it had been surfaced and traded explicitly, a co branded marketing event, a preferred partner mention in the school's own programme, in exchange for a better price. The negotiators in this exercise did land inside the zone of possible agreement, closing at six dollars fifty five a pound, a workable outcome. But they closed close to the seller's preferred end of the range rather than the buyer's, and the debrief was blunt about why: the halo value of the hospitality school connection was mentioned only in passing, never explicitly traded for a lower number, and the seller's opening anchor of nearly eight dollars likely pulled the final price higher than it needed to land, exactly the anchoring effect this episode has already described.

The third negotiation is the one that shows what a fully worked multi issue deal actually looks like, and it is worth walking through in some detail because the arithmetic is the whole lesson. Two teams negotiated a cartoon licensing deal, a buyer and a seller, across four separate issues at once, price per episode, the number of broadcast runs per episode, when payment would actually be made, and a deliberately open bucket called Other Terms, covering merchandising, syndication and future rights. The asymmetry that made the whole negotiation integrative rather than distributive was simple and, once seen, obvious: the seller had had a difficult year and was cash strapped, so they needed payment now, while the buyer had cash on hand and genuinely did not care whether payment landed this year or years later. That single difference in what each side actually valued turned what looked like a price fight into a trade. The buyer offered a higher headline price in exchange for deferring payment to year five instead of year one, a concession that cost the buyer almost nothing in present value terms and solved the seller's real, urgent problem. Layer in the other two issues, more broadcast runs per episode, which the buyer valued heavily for advertising inventory and the seller barely valued at all, and rights bundled into the Other Terms bucket, merchandising, syndication, worth real money to the buyer and costing the seller very little since those rights were, for them, uncertain future income rather than money already in hand. The team that logrolled across all four issues rather than fighting over price alone landed a deal worth several times more than a status quo, price only agreement would have produced, turning what a single issue negotiation would have settled at a modest, break even value into an outcome worth millions more once every issue was allowed onto the table and traded according to what each side actually needed rather than what each side initially said they wanted.

Run all three negotiations back through the frameworks from earlier in this episode and the pattern is unmistakable. The salary negotiation stayed distributive because nobody asked why. The coffee negotiation found half of its integrative value and left the other half unspoken. The cartoon deal found nearly all of it, because the negotiators went looking for asymmetry on purpose, asked what each side valued and did not value, and traded accordingly. The tool was identical in all three cases. The only thing that changed was whether anyone actually used it.

Pause here and try this yourself, one more time, because this is the exercise that would have saved half the value in the very first story. Before your next real negotiation, write down, honestly, what you believe the other side's underlying interest is, not their stated position, their actual interest, and write down at least one issue beyond the headline number that you could plausibly add to the table. If you cannot fill in either blank, you are not ready to negotiate yet, you are only ready to haggle, and haggling is what happened in that first story, at roughly half the value that was actually available.

Now, a handful of traps worth naming directly, because they catch capable people again and again. The first is treating every negotiation as automatically single issue, price and nothing else, when a second or third issue is very often sitting right there, unexamined, simply because nobody asked what else might be on the table. The second is confusing an aggressive opening number with a strong position, when the two are entirely different things. A number said loudly from a weak BATNA is not strength, it is theatre, and experienced negotiators on the other side of the table can usually tell the difference within a few minutes. The third is revealing your own reservation point too early or too plainly, which caps your own upside the moment the other side realises exactly how far you are willing to move. The fourth is confusing your aspiration, what you would love to get, with your BATNA, what you will actually do if this deal fails, and letting hope quietly stand in for a real fallback that was never actually built. And the fifth, perhaps the most expensive of all in practice, is declaring a zone of possible agreement empty the moment the first two numbers fail to overlap, without ever trying to restructure the deal, add an issue, change the timing, change the volume, before walking away from something that might genuinely have worked.

A sixth trap deserves its own mention, because it shows up constantly in family and small business negotiations where the two sides expect to deal with each other again. Winning every single point on paper while damaging the relationship is very often a worse outcome than a slightly smaller win that leaves both sides willing to negotiate with you again next year. The director in our first story got his six crore, but the case notes are blunt that the relationship soured, an ultimatum tends to do that, and a producer who feels cornered rather than persuaded is a producer who remembers it at the next renewal. Ask, before you press an advantage all the way to its limit, whether you are pricing in the cost of the next negotiation, because there is very often a next one, and the reputation you build in this one negotiates on your behalf before you have said a word in the next.

Now, quickly, the whole episode in miniature before we close. Power comes from alternatives, so build your BATNA before you build your argument. The zone of possible agreement can be empty, and walking away from an empty zone is the system working, not failing, provided you tried to restructure the deal first. Positions are what people say, interests are why they say it, and four words, why do you want that, will do more work than any clever counter offer. Logrolling turns single issue wars into multi issue markets, so always look for a second issue before you sit down. Anchor first when you are informed and legitimate, and answer an unreasonable anchor with objective criteria rather than outrage. And when you disagree about the future, stop arguing and bet, because a contingent contract resolves the disagreement and tells you, honestly, who actually believed their own forecast.

Your one sentence for managing negotiations: build your alternatives before the meeting, negotiate interests rather than positions, and always look for a second issue, because a negotiation with only one number on the table can only ever produce a winner and a loser, while a negotiation with several can produce two of them.
