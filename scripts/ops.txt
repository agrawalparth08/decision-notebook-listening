It is eleven at night in a small hostel room that smells of butter and vanilla essence, and two roommates are staring at a single electric oven the way sailors stare at a storm. The phone on the desk has rung eleven times in the last hour. Each ring is an order for cookies, warm, boxed, delivered within the hour, because that is the promise they made when they started this small business between classes. One roommate is mixing dough as fast as her arms will let her, flour on her elbows, the bowl going around and around. The other is standing by the oven door, watching the little window, willing the tray inside to bake faster through pure concentration, which, it turns out, does not work on ovens.

They are behind. Orders are piling up on the desk, scrawled on paper, and customers are calling back asking where their cookies are. So they do what any two panicked people would do. They buy a second mixing bowl. They recruit a friend from down the hall to help load trays. They argue about whether to double the recipe. And none of it helps. The cookies still come out at the same rate they always did, one tray every ten minutes, because there is exactly one oven in the room and it does not care how many people are mixing dough on the other side of the kitchen. The queue in front of the oven simply gets longer, now with extra hands standing around it, watching dough wait its turn.

Somewhere around midnight, one of them finally says the sentence that changes everything. She says, it does not matter how fast we mix if the oven only holds one tray. Whatever we do to the mixing, the front door of this business is the oven, and only the oven. And the moment she says it, the whole frantic evening reorganises itself in her head. They stop hiring more mixers. They stop buying second bowls. They start asking a completely different question, which is how do we get more out of the oven itself, because that is the only lever in the room that actually moves the number of cookies they can sell tonight.

Hello, and welcome to the Decision Notebook listening library. Today, operations management. This is a subject about one thing above all others, which is flow, how work moves through a system that has limits, and where those limits actually sit. In the next while we will walk through the bottleneck and why speeding up the wrong station changes nothing, Little's Law and why it never lies, the strange mathematics of queues and why a resource running at full utilisation is quietly manufacturing delay, and the newsvendor problem, which is the discipline of ordering something that will spoil. We will sit inside the case of a two-student cookie business properly, and inside a workforce case about mechanics of different skill levels, and by the end you will never look at a queue, a kitchen, or a factory floor the same way again.

Now here is the thing about operations as a subject. Every other course in this programme tends to ask what should we do. Operations asks a quieter, more mechanical question first, which is what actually happens when work flows through the system we already have. And that question turns out to be strangely universal. It does not matter whether the system is a dorm room kitchen, a hospital ward, a steel plant, or a call centre. Work arrives, it moves through a sequence of steps, some steps are faster than others, and the slowest step, whatever it happens to be, decides how much the whole system can produce. Everything else in the subject, queues, inventory, workforce planning, is really commentary on that one central fact.

This is why operations feels different from strategy or marketing, where the question is usually about choice, about which market to enter or which customer to chase. Operations assumes the choice has already been made, the business exists, the orders are coming in, and now asks the more humble question of whether the machine underneath that business can actually deliver. And here is the uncomfortable truth the subject keeps returning to. Most managers, when a system is too slow, reach instinctively for effort. Work harder, hire more people, buy more equipment, everywhere at once. And most of the time that instinct is expensive and wrong, because effort applied to the wrong part of the system produces nothing except a longer queue somewhere else. The entire discipline of operations management can be summarised as learning to find the one place where effort actually converts into output, and refusing to spend energy anywhere else. Hold onto that thought, because every framework in this episode is a different tool for finding that one place.

Let us start with the framework that the cold open already gave away, because it is the single most important idea in the whole course. The bottleneck, sometimes called the theory of constraints. In plain words it says this. Any system that processes things through a sequence of steps can only go as fast as its slowest step, and improving any step other than the slowest one does nothing to the system's overall speed. The slowest step is the bottleneck, and the bottleneck sets the pace for everyone.

Think about it in the most ordinary setting you can imagine, a narrow lane with one gate at the end that only lets one car through every few seconds. It does not matter how wide the lane is before the gate, or how fast the cars are driving toward it. The gate decides how many cars leave the lane per minute, and if you widen the lane further back, you have only built a longer parking lot in front of the gate.

Now the business version, and this is exactly where the cookie business lives, the case that anchors this entire subject. Two roommates running a cookie business out of a dorm kitchen. Let us walk through the timing properly, because the numbers matter. Mixing the dough for one order takes six minutes. Loading the mixed dough onto a tray takes two minutes. Baking takes ten minutes, and it happens in the oven, one tray at a time, because there is only one oven. Cooling and packing the finished cookies takes a few minutes more. Now ask the question that decides the whole business. How many orders can this kitchen complete in an hour.

The tempting answer is to add up the times, and conclude that each order takes about twenty minutes from start to finish, so the shop can do three orders an hour. That answer is wrong, and it is wrong for a reason that matters far beyond cookies. The mixing station and the oven do not have to wait for each other in lockstep. While one tray bakes in the oven for ten minutes, the roommates can already be mixing the next order's dough, six minutes, well within that window. So mixing never actually holds up the line, because it always finishes before the oven is free again. The only station that cannot be sped up, doubled, or worked around is the oven, because there is exactly one of it, and it takes ten minutes per tray no matter what else is happening in the kitchen. One tray every ten minutes means six trays an hour. That is the ceiling. That is the whole business's true production rate, and it has nothing to do with how fast anyone mixes.

This is why the frantic decisions in our cold open failed. A second mixing bowl does not help, because mixing was never the constraint. A third roommate helping load trays does not help, because loading was never the constraint either. Buy a faster spoon, hire ten more people to knead dough, and the kitchen still produces exactly six orders an hour, because the oven bakes at exactly the same speed regardless of how much dough is queued up waiting for it. The only way to sell a seventh order in that hour is to change something about the oven itself, get a second oven, use a bigger tray that holds more cookies per bake, or find some way to shorten the ten minutes. Everything else is, as the roommates eventually realised at midnight, decoration.

When do you reach for this framework. Any time output feels stuck despite people working hard, and especially any time someone proposes adding resources to fix a slow process. Before agreeing, ask one question. Where exactly is the queue building up. Because work in progress always piles up directly in front of the bottleneck, never anywhere else, and that pile is the system pointing directly at the answer. Walk the floor of any operation, physical or digital, follow the trail of waiting inventory, waiting emails, waiting patients, and you will walk straight to the constraint without needing a single spreadsheet.

So remember. In any system with a sequence of steps, the slowest step sets the pace for the whole system, and effort spent anywhere else is invisible to the customer. Find the constraint, improve the constraint, and only the constraint.

Pause here and try this yourself. A small print shop has three steps for making a poster order. Designing the layout takes five minutes. Printing takes fifteen minutes on the one large format printer they own. Trimming and packaging takes four minutes. How many posters can this shop produce in an hour, and which step should the owner invest in if she wants to grow. Work it out before I say it out loud.

Here is the answer talked through. The printer takes fifteen minutes per poster, and there is only one printer, so nothing else in the shop matters for the ceiling. Sixty minutes divided by fifteen minutes gives four posters an hour, and that is true even though designing and trimming are both much faster and could easily keep up with a faster printer if one existed. If the owner wants to grow, hiring a second designer changes nothing, the posters will simply queue in front of the printer a little sooner. The only investment that raises the ceiling is a second printer, or a faster one, or finding a way to print more than one poster per run. That is the bottleneck framework working exactly as it should.

Now let us move to the second law of this subject, and it is almost embarrassingly simple once you see it, which is exactly why it is so powerful. Little's Law. In plain words it says that the number of things sitting inside a system at any moment equals the rate at which things arrive, multiplied by the average time each thing spends inside. That is the entire law. Three quantities, and if you know any two of them, the third one is no longer a mystery, it is arithmetic.

Take the most everyday version of this you can imagine, a coffee shop near your office. Customers arrive at a steady rate, say sixty an hour, and each one spends about eight minutes inside, ordering, waiting, collecting their cup, maybe sitting for a moment. Little's Law says that at any given instant, whether you glance in at nine in the morning or at noon, there are on average eight people inside that coffee shop. Not roughly eight, not usually eight, but eight, always, as long as the arrival rate and the time inside stay at those levels. This is not a guess, it is a mathematical identity, and it holds for any stable system you can imagine, a hospital ward, an email inbox, a court system processing cases, a factory floor covered in half finished units.

Now the business use, and this is where the law becomes a genuine management tool rather than a curiosity. Imagine a hospital emergency room that admits ninety patients a day, and imagine the average patient occupies a bed for six hours before being discharged or moved to a ward. Using Little's Law, the hospital administrator can immediately calculate how many beds the emergency room needs on average, without commissioning a single consulting study. Ninety patients a day is three point seven five patients an hour. Multiply by six hours, and you get roughly twenty two and a half beds occupied at any given moment, on average. If the ward only has fifteen beds, the administrator now knows, with nothing more than two numbers and a multiplication, that this emergency room is structurally short of capacity, and patients will be waiting in corridors on a regular basis, not because anyone is doing their job badly, but because the arithmetic simply does not close.

The other beautiful use of the law is diagnostic. If you can count how many things are currently sitting inside a system, and you know the arrival rate, you can work out the average time things spend in the system, even if nobody is timing anything with a stopwatch. Count the emails sitting in a shared inbox, count how many new emails arrive per day, and Little's Law tells you, honestly and without excuses, how long an email typically waits before someone answers it. Managers who want to know their own turnaround time, without asking anyone to fill in a survey, can simply count.

When do you reach for this framework. Whenever you want to know how much work in progress a system is carrying, or how long something typically waits inside a process, and you cannot or do not want to time it directly. It is the fastest audit tool in the entire subject, a stopwatch and a headcount, nothing more.

So remember. The number of things inside a system, the rate they arrive, and the time they spend inside are locked together by one simple multiplication, and knowing any two tells you the third, with no exceptions, ever.

Pause here and try this yourself. A bank branch serves forty customers an hour on average, and a manager, watching the floor at a random moment, counts ten customers currently inside the branch, in the queue or at the counter. According to Little's Law, how long is the average customer spending inside the branch. Work it out before I say it.

Here is the answer talked through. Little's Law says the number inside equals the arrival rate times the time inside. We know the number inside is ten, and the arrival rate is forty an hour. So ten equals forty multiplied by the time inside, which means the time inside equals ten divided by forty, or one quarter of an hour, fifteen minutes. The manager did not need to time a single customer with a stopwatch to know that. She only needed to count heads and know the arrival rate, and the law handed her the answer.

Let us move to the third framework, and this one is the reason experienced operations people never celebrate a system running at one hundred percent capacity. Variability, and the strange behaviour of queues.

Here is the plain words version. Even when a system has, on average, enough capacity to handle its average demand, queues still form, and they form because the world does not arrive in neat, evenly spaced intervals. Customers bunch up. Some orders take longer than expected, some patients are more complicated than others, some machines jam for no obvious reason. This unevenness is called variability, and it is the quiet tax that every real system pays, a tax that a spreadsheet built on averages will never show you.

The everyday example almost everyone has lived through is a doctor's clinic. The doctor is booked from nine in the morning to five in the evening, back to back appointments, each slot exactly fifteen minutes, and on paper the schedule fits perfectly inside the working day, capacity exactly matches demand. And yet by four in the afternoon the doctor is running two hours late. Why. Because one patient at eleven o'clock had a complicated case that took forty minutes instead of fifteen, and there was no gap anywhere in the schedule to absorb that overrun. The delay does not shrink as the day goes on, it accumulates, because every fully booked slot after that one complicated patient inherits the lateness of the slot before it. The schedule had capacity for an average day, and it got a real day instead, which is never average, it is always lumpy.

Now the deeper business truth, and this is the part that surprises people the first time they hear it. As a resource's utilisation approaches one hundred percent, waiting time does not increase gently, in a straight line. It explodes, curving sharply upward as utilisation gets close to full. A system running at seventy percent utilisation might have short, tolerable queues. The same system pushed to ninety five percent utilisation, only a modest increase in busyness, can see its average wait times multiply many times over, because there is no longer any slack left anywhere to absorb the natural unevenness of arrivals and service times. This is why call centres that are staffed to exactly match average call volume are always, always overwhelmed during the actual busy periods, and why airport security lines that look fine most of the day become nightmares the moment one flight's worth of passengers arrives together.

The business example with numbers, still inside the world of the cookie business. Suppose on an average night the roommates receive exactly six orders an hour, precisely matching their oven's capacity of six orders an hour. It sounds perfectly balanced on paper. In reality it is a recipe for disaster, because real orders do not arrive exactly ten minutes apart, evenly spaced, forever. Some minutes bring two calls at once, some bring none. The moment two orders arrive close together, a queue starts forming in front of the oven, and because the oven is already running at its absolute maximum on average, there is no spare capacity anywhere in the system to clear that queue before the next cluster of orders arrives. The kitchen that looks perfectly sized on a spreadsheet is, in practice, permanently a few orders behind by ten at night.

When do you reach for this framework. Any time someone proposes running a resource, a machine, a team, a doctor's calendar, at full utilisation as a sign of efficiency. That is precisely the moment to ask what happens to waiting time when reality turns out to be less tidy than the average. Slack, an empty appointment slot, an underused machine hour, a team member who is not booked solid, is not waste sitting on the balance sheet. It is the shock absorber that keeps the whole system from seizing up the day something does not go exactly to plan, which is to say, most days.

So remember. Queues form even when average capacity exceeds average demand, because arrivals and service times are never perfectly even, and pushing utilisation toward one hundred percent does not buy you efficiency, it buys you an explosion in waiting time the very first time something takes longer than expected.

Now the fourth framework, and it might be the single most elegant small model taught anywhere in this programme. The newsvendor problem, named for the classic image of a person selling newspapers on a street corner, who has to decide every morning how many papers to buy before knowing how many will actually sell that day.

In plain words, the newsvendor problem applies to anything perishable, anything that must be ordered or produced before demand is known, and that loses most or all of its value if it is not sold in time. Fresh bread, newspapers, fashion clothing for a season, flowers for a wedding, seats on an airline flight before it takes off. Order too little, and you turn away paying customers, a cost economists call underage. Order too much, and you are left holding unsold stock that has to be discounted, discarded, or written off, a cost called overage. The entire decision comes down to balancing these two very different kinds of pain against each other.

Here is the everyday version. Imagine you are running a small stall at a college festival selling samosas, and you have to decide this morning, before the crowd shows up, how many to prepare. Let us say each samosa costs you ten rupees to make, and you sell it for thirty rupees, so if you run short, every unsold customer represents twenty rupees of profit you never collected, that is your underage cost. But if you make too many and the festival winds down with samosas left over, each unsold samosa is a pure loss of the ten rupees you spent making it, that is your overage cost. Notice something important here. The cost of running short, twenty rupees of lost profit, is twice as large as the cost of making too many, ten rupees of wasted ingredients. Because underage hurts more than overage in this example, the logic of the newsvendor says you should lean toward ordering generously, accepting a higher chance of a few leftovers in exchange for rarely turning away a paying customer.

This balancing act has a name, the critical ratio, and it gives you a precise cutoff, though the intuition alone will already carry you a long way in daily decisions. The critical ratio is the underage cost divided by the sum of underage and overage costs. In the samosa example, underage is twenty rupees and overage is ten rupees, so the critical ratio is twenty divided by thirty, which is roughly zero point six seven, sixty seven percent. What that number tells you is this. You should keep increasing your order quantity until there is only about a thirty three percent chance of having leftovers, because beyond that point the risk of running out has become smaller than the risk of overstocking, and it is no longer worth pushing the order higher. The higher the critical ratio, the more generously you should order, because it means running out hurts far more than having leftovers.

Now flip the story, because the newsvendor problem cuts both ways, and this is where businesses often get it wrong by applying the same instinct everywhere. Imagine instead you are ordering fresh flowers for a wedding hall, where each unused flower is a complete write off, discarded within a day, but flowers are cheap relative to what a wedding client pays for the arrangement. If overage cost is high relative to underage cost, the critical ratio shrinks, and the correct decision is to order conservatively, accepting a higher chance of running slightly short rather than drowning in wasted, wilted stock. The mathematics is identical in both cases, only the two costs have swapped their relative sizes, and the decision swings the other way entirely.

When do you reach for this framework. Any time you are deciding a quantity in advance of uncertain demand, for something that cannot simply be reordered later once you see how the day is going. Restaurants deciding how much to prep, retailers placing a single seasonal order months before the season starts, event planners booking catering headcounts. Before you decide, ask the two cost questions explicitly. What does it truly cost me if I run short, and what does it truly cost me if I have leftovers. Whichever cost is larger pulls the decision toward it, and the critical ratio simply makes that pull precise.

So remember. Perishable ordering decisions are never about guessing the exact right number, they are about weighing the pain of running out against the pain of having leftovers, and letting whichever pain is larger pull your order quantity toward it.

Pause here and try this yourself. A small bakery sells fresh cakes that must be thrown away if unsold by the end of the day. Each cake costs the bakery one hundred rupees to make and sells for two hundred and fifty rupees. Work out the underage cost, the overage cost, and the critical ratio, and say in plain words whether the bakery should lean toward ordering generously or conservatively.

Here is the answer talked through. If a cake is not made and a customer wanted one, the bakery loses the profit on that sale, two hundred and fifty minus one hundred, which is one hundred and fifty rupees, that is the underage cost. If a cake is made and not sold, the bakery loses the full one hundred rupees it cost to make, that is the overage cost. The critical ratio is underage divided by underage plus overage, one hundred and fifty divided by two hundred and fifty, which is zero point six, sixty percent. Because sixty percent is comfortably above the halfway point, running short is meaningfully more painful than having leftovers, so the bakery should lean toward ordering generously, and should be comfortable accepting a somewhat higher chance of a few unsold cakes at closing time in exchange for rarely disappointing a customer.

Now the final piece of the framework toolkit, and it comes from a case in the course about a workforce of mechanics, which teaches a lesson about capacity that has nothing to do with machines and everything to do with people. The lesson is this. Skills do not add up evenly, they multiply the value of the people who have them, and this changes how you should think about headcount.

In plain words, when a workforce contains people of different skill levels, simply counting heads badly understates or overstates real capacity, because a less experienced worker is not a slightly slower version of an expert, they can be a very different fraction of one. In the mechanics case, a worker classified at a lower skill level, level four, was found to take about two and a half times as long to complete the same repair as an expert mechanic. That is not a small gap. It means two level four mechanics do not equal one expert, they equal something closer to eighty percent of one expert's output, because two and a half times slower means each one produces roughly forty percent of an expert's output on their own.

The everyday version of this idea is obvious once you say it out loud. Imagine assigning a house move to four people who have never done it before, versus two people who move furniture for a living. The four amateurs will spend enormous energy, drop things, argue about the best way to carry the sofa, and quite possibly take longer overall than the two professionals working in practiced rhythm. More hands is not the same as more capability.

The business consequence in the mechanics case was that simply hiring more level four mechanics to hit a headcount target, the obvious, easy answer to a capacity shortfall, did not actually close the gap in output the company needed. What worked instead was cross-training, investing in moving mechanics up the skill ladder, and building a better mix of skill levels rather than a bigger pile of junior ones. A workforce with the right mix of experience often beats a larger workforce of the wrong mix, at a lower total cost, because the multiplier on skill is doing more work than the simple arithmetic of headcount would suggest.

When do you reach for this framework. Any time a capacity problem is being solved by adding people rather than by asking what those people can actually do relative to your best performers. Before approving a hiring plan to fix a bottleneck, ask the mechanics question. What is the actual output ratio between our most and least experienced people doing this task, and would training existing staff close the gap faster and cheaper than hiring more of the slower kind.

So remember. Headcount is not capacity, skill is capacity, and cross-training the people you already have often beats hiring more people at the skill level you are trying to escape.

Let us sit inside the two central cases of this course properly now, because the numbers only come alive once you see the whole story.

Return to that hostel kitchen at eleven at night, because there is more to the cookie business story than the bottleneck alone, and the case rewards a second look. Once the roommates realise the oven is their true constraint, they do not stop there, because the case pushes them, and pushes you, to ask a series of follow up questions that every operations manager eventually has to ask about their own bottleneck. Can the bottleneck be run more of the time. If the oven currently sits idle between orders because dough is not ready when a tray finishes, then simply staggering the mixing schedule so a new tray is always ready the instant the oven opens its door effectively increases output without buying anything new, because idle bottleneck time is output quietly leaking away. Can the bottleneck do more per cycle. If the oven can fit more than one tray at a time, or a slightly larger tray holding more cookies per order, the ten minute cycle now produces more per bake, and the whole system's ceiling rises without a single extra machine purchased. Can work be moved away from the bottleneck entirely. If some part of what currently happens inside the ten minute baking window, like final packaging, could instead happen after the tray comes out, on a separate counter, using separate hands, then the oven is freed to spend its precious ten minutes doing only what only an oven can do, baking.

And here is the sting in the case that students remember years later. The roommates' first instinct, the instinct of most managers under pressure, was to work harder and buy more resources everywhere at once. A second bowl, a third pair of hands, more effort thrown at the entire system uniformly. The case's real lesson is that uniform effort against a system with one true constraint is not merely inefficient, it is close to useless, because ninety percent of that effort lands on parts of the kitchen that were never short of capacity to begin with. The oven does not care how many people are standing around the mixing bowl. It bakes one tray every ten minutes whether the kitchen is calm or chaotic, and the only conversation worth having, once you understand this, is the conversation about the oven itself.

Now the second case, the workforce of mechanics, and this one plays out on a very different stage, a repair floor rather than a dorm kitchen, but the underlying discipline of operations management turns out to be strangely similar. A company running a fleet maintenance operation is short on repair capacity. Machines are waiting too long to get serviced, customers are unhappy, and the obvious, almost reflexive answer on the table is to hire more mechanics. But which mechanics, and how many, is not obvious once you actually look at the skill data. The case lays out mechanics across several skill levels, from the most junior apprentices up through fully qualified experts, and the timing data reveals something the headcount spreadsheet completely hides. A worker at the lowest measured skill level, level four in the case's own terminology, takes about two and a half times as long as an expert to complete the identical repair job. Put twenty level four mechanics on the floor, and in raw output terms you have something closer to eight expert equivalents, not twenty, because output is capped by speed as much as by warm bodies standing at workstations.

The naive plan, hire fifteen more level four mechanics to eliminate the backlog, looks attractive on a spreadsheet that only counts heads, and it is expensive, because level four mechanics still draw a salary even though their output per head is a fraction of an expert's. The case walks through the alternative, cross-training existing mechanics to move up the skill ladder, and rebalancing the mix of skills already on the floor rather than simply adding more of the slowest tier. The company that solves this problem well ends up needing meaningfully fewer total heads than the naive hiring plan would have required, at a lower total wage bill, because it bought capability instead of buying bodies. The deeper lesson that both cases share, the cookie kitchen and the mechanics floor, despite looking nothing alike on the surface, is that capacity is never a single number you can read off a headcount report. It lives in the specific bottleneck, the specific skill mix, the specific place where the system actually strains, and finding that place honestly is worth more than any amount of generic extra effort thrown at the whole operation at once.

Now let us talk about the traps, because operations management has a small number of misconceptions that catch even careful, intelligent people, over and over again.

The first trap is treating every improvement as equally valuable. A manager sees three ways to speed up a process and implements all three, feeling productive, when in fact only one of them touched the actual bottleneck, and the other two were wasted motion dressed up as progress. Always ask first, before improving anything, whether the thing you are about to improve is the constraint, because improvement anywhere else is invisible to the customer no matter how satisfying it feels to the manager doing it.

The second trap is worshipping full utilisation. A machine sitting idle for twenty minutes a day looks like waste on a factory dashboard, and a manager under pressure to show efficiency schedules it to run every possible minute. But we have already seen what happens to waiting time as utilisation climbs toward one hundred percent, it does not rise gently, it explodes, and a system with zero slack anywhere cannot absorb a single surprise without falling behind for the rest of the day. Some idle capacity is not waste, it is the very thing that keeps a system stable when reality refuses to behave like the average case in a spreadsheet.

The third trap is applying newsvendor logic with the wrong pair of numbers, or worse, with no numbers at all, ordering purely on gut feeling or on what was ordered last week. Every perishable ordering decision has an underage cost and an overage cost hiding inside it, and skipping the exercise of naming both explicitly, even roughly, means the order quantity is being set by habit rather than by the actual economics of the situation.

The fourth trap is confusing headcount with capacity, exactly as the mechanics case demonstrates. A staffing plan that only counts warm bodies, without asking how much each body can actually produce relative to your best people, will consistently overspend on the wrong kind of hiring and underinvest in training the people already on the floor.

And the final trap, perhaps the quietest one, is assuming that because a system worked fine on an average day, it will keep working fine, forever, without any slack built in anywhere. Averages are a summary of history, not a promise about tomorrow, and the whole discipline of thinking about variability exists because tomorrow is reliably less tidy than the average of all the days that came before it.

Let us bring this home. Operations management, underneath all its cases and its charts, is really the discipline of finding the one true limit in a system and respecting it, rather than scattering effort evenly across a process and hoping something sticks. The bottleneck sets the pace, and nothing else in the system matters for output until the bottleneck itself changes. Little's Law is not an approximation, it is an identity, the number of things inside a system always equals the arrival rate multiplied by the time each thing spends inside, and you can use it to audit any process with nothing more than a stopwatch and a headcount. Variability is the quiet tax that turns a system that looks perfectly sized on paper into one that is chronically behind in practice, and pushing utilisation toward one hundred percent does not buy efficiency, it buys fragility. The newsvendor problem teaches you to name the two costs, running short and running over, explicitly, and to let whichever one hurts more pull your order quantity toward it. And the mechanics case reminds you that capacity lives in skill, not in headcount, and that cross-training your own people is very often the cheapest capacity expansion available to you.

Your one sentence for operations management is this. Find the true constraint, respect what Little's Law tells you about flow, leave room for the unevenness of real life, and size every perishable decision by weighing what it costs to run short against what it costs to have leftovers.
