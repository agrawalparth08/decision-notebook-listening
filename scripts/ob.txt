Picture a mid sized firm, the kind with an open floor plan and a coffee machine everyone complains about. There are two people on the analytics team worth knowing about for this story. One of them, call him Sanjay, is the person everyone in the building quietly relies on to be right. He has been at the company nine years, he has watched every kind of model break in every way a model can break, and when he tells you a forecast is wrong, it is wrong. He is also, if we are honest about him, hard to sit next to. He interrupts. He does not remember your name if you have spoken to him fewer than four times. When a colleague brings him a half finished spreadsheet, he does not say thank you for trying, he says why is this wrong.

Down the row sits Meera. Meera is pleasant, quick with a compliment, remembers everyone's birthday, and is, by most technical measures, an average analyst. Nothing wrong with her work. Nothing remarkable either.

Now watch what happens when a new hire gets stuck on a tricky attribution model at eleven at night before a client deadline. He does not walk over to Sanjay, who could solve it in four minutes. He messages Meera, who is warm and easy to approach, who spends forty minutes working through it with him at half the speed Sanjay would have managed, and gets it half right.

This happens again the next week. And the week after that. Not because anyone doubts Sanjay's ability. Everybody in that office, asked privately, would tell you Sanjay is the sharper mind. But knowing that and acting on it turn out to be two separate things, and under time pressure, likeability wins the vote every single time expertise does not force its way into the room by itself.

Multiply that one small daily choice by a hundred people over a year, and you get an organisation that is quietly, steadily failing to use its own best knowledge. Nobody decided this. No memo was written. It happened one small preference at a time, in exactly the way most puzzling things happen inside organisations, not through conspiracy, but through the ordinary wiring of how people behave when nobody is checking on the wiring.

That is the subject for today. Why capable, well meaning people, dropped into an ordinary office, produce outcomes nobody would have designed on purpose.

If there is one thread holding today's episode together, it is this. Organisational behaviour is not really about fixing people. It is about noticing the invisible arithmetic that already governs how people choose, and adjusting that arithmetic, rather than lecturing the person stuck inside it.

Think about how often a manager's honest first reaction to a problem is a character judgement. He is lazy. She does not care about this team. He is just difficult. Those are verdicts about a soul. And they are almost always wrong, or at least beside the point, because behaviour at work is rarely a pure expression of who someone is. It is far more often the visible output of a hidden calculation: what do I believe will happen if I act, what do I believe I will get if it works, and does the getting matter to me. Change any term in that calculation and the behaviour changes with it, often within days, with no change in character required at all.

This is a genuinely different way of looking at people than most of us grew up with. Most of us were trained by school, by family, by the general folklore of the workplace, to sort behaviour into good workers and bad workers, motivated people and lazy people, team players and difficult ones. Organisational behaviour, the discipline, asks you to set that sorting aside and instead ask a narrower, more useful question. Given what this person believes about their situation right now, is their behaviour actually the sensible response? Nine times out of ten, the honest answer is yes. The forecasting analyst who stopped volunteering for stretch projects is not lazy, he tried twice, was passed over both times for a favourite, and correctly concluded that trying does not change outcomes here. The junior consultant who stopped speaking up in client meetings is not shy, she spoke up once, was quietly overruled and made to look foolish in front of the client, and correctly concluded that speaking up is expensive here.

Notice what this reframing buys you. It turns every people problem from a verdict into a diagnosis. A verdict ends the conversation, you write the person off, you replace them, and eighteen months later you have hired their replacement and are watching the exact same behaviour appear again, because the arithmetic in the room never changed. A diagnosis keeps the conversation open. It asks what belief, what experience, what piece of the environment is producing this response, and it points you toward a lever you can actually move.

Keep that thread in mind through everything that follows today. Every framework we are about to walk through, motivation, satisfaction, judgement, likeability, is really just a more precise name for one piece of that hidden arithmetic. Learn to see the arithmetic, and half of what looks like a mystery about people stops being mysterious at all.

Let us start with motivation, because it is the piece managers get wrong most expensively, most often, and with the best intentions.

The cleanest model of motivation in the whole field is called expectancy theory, and it says something quite precise. Motivation is not a mood and it is not a personality trait. It is the product, the multiplication, of three separate beliefs a person is carrying around in their head. The first belief: can I actually do this, if I put in the effort. The second belief: if I do it well, will that performance actually be rewarded. The third belief: do I even care about the reward on offer. Multiply those three together and you get motivation. Add any one of them to zero and the whole product collapses to zero, no matter how strong the other two are.

That multiplication, rather than addition, is the entire insight, and it is worth sitting with because it is counterintuitive. We tend to think of motivation as a stack, more of this factor plus more of that factor equals more drive. Expectancy theory says no, it is a chain, and a chain is only as strong as its weakest link, not its average link.

In everyday language, think about a teenager and their exam preparation. Say the subject is a language they find genuinely hard, one they doubt they can pass no matter how many hours they put in. It does not matter how much you promise them for a good grade, a new phone, a trip, anything, because the first belief, can I do this, is sitting near zero. All the reward in the world multiplied by near zero effort belief still gives you near zero motivation. Now flip it. Say they are confident they can pass easily, and the reward on offer is something they genuinely want. But suppose past experience has taught them that no matter how they perform, their parents forget the promised reward, or move the goalposts afterward. Now the middle belief, if I do it will I actually get it, is the broken link, and again the product falls apart, even though this teenager is perfectly capable and the reward would matter to them if they believed it was coming.

Now take that into a business setting, because this is where the framework earns its keep. A sales manager cannot understand why her best performer from last year has gone quiet this year. The bonus structure has not changed. The targets are, if anything, more achievable than before. On paper, everything that should motivate this person is still in place. But look closer, and you discover that last year's top prize, the recognition trip, the public credit, went to the boss's favourite, someone who was, by the numbers, not actually the strongest performer. The quiet employee did the arithmetic once, watched the outcome, and updated a belief permanently: if I perform, it will not reliably be rewarded here. That is the middle term of the equation going to zero. No pep talk fixes this. No slightly larger bonus fixes this. What has to be fixed is the actual, demonstrated, trustworthy link between performance and reward, in full view of the team, more than once, before that belief will move back up from zero.

So when should you actually reach for this framework? Anytime someone capable has visibly stopped trying, and the obvious explanations, laziness, distraction, a bad attitude, feel too easy or do not fit what you already know about the person. Expectancy theory gives you a three question diagnostic instead of a character judgement. Ask, honestly, which of the three beliefs is missing. Do they doubt they can do it, in which case the fix is training, coaching, smaller steps, a mentor. Do they doubt it will be rewarded if they do it, in which case the fix is fixing the actual link between performance and outcome, visibly and repeatedly. Or do they simply not care about what is on offer, in which case the fix is finding out what they do care about, because the reward menu, not the person, is the problem.

So remember: motivation is not addition, it is multiplication, can I, will I be rewarded, do I care, and when someone capable stops trying, do not look for a character flaw, look for the zero.

Pause here and try this yourself. Think of someone in your own team, or even yourself, who used to put in real effort on something and has quietly stopped. Before you read on, silently walk through the three questions. Do they believe they are capable of it. Do they believe good effort actually leads to the outcome they want here. Do they actually want that outcome. Now here is how a coach in this course would talk through it with you. Most people, when they do this exercise honestly, are surprised to find the block sits in the middle term, not the first or the third. We assume disengaged people do not care, or cannot do the work, but far more often they have simply run the experiment once, tried hard, watched the reward go somewhere else or nowhere at all, and rationally stopped repeating an experiment that did not pay off. If that is what you found, the fix is not a speech about caring more. It is rebuilding, visibly, the link between effort and outcome, and letting them watch it hold at least twice before you expect belief to return.

Now to the second puzzle, and it explains something almost every manager has lived through personally: the raise that stopped working.

This comes from Frederick Herzberg's two factor theory, and the core claim is almost embarrassingly simple once you hear it, though it overturns a lot of ordinary management instinct. Herzberg says the things that cause dissatisfaction at work and the things that cause real motivation are not opposite ends of the same scale. They are two entirely separate lists, doing two entirely separate jobs.

The first list, Herzberg calls hygiene factors. Pay, working conditions, company policy, job security, the relationship with your immediate boss. When these are poor, people are miserable, and fixing them removes the misery. But here is the twist: fixing them only brings you back to neutral. It does not, on its own, produce drive, energy, or commitment. Hygiene factors can only take away pain. They cannot manufacture desire.

The second list is completely different: meaningful work, recognition for a job well done, real responsibility, a sense of growth or advancement. Herzberg calls these motivators, and they are the only things on the entire list capable of producing genuine extra effort, the discretionary kind nobody can force out of you with a policy.

An everyday example makes this land quickly. Think about a flat you live in. If the roof leaks, if the water pressure is unreliable, if the landlord ignores your complaints, you are unhappy, and fixing all three of those things will make you stop being unhappy. But fixing the leak does not make you love the flat, does not make you tell your friends how wonderful it is to live there, does not make you want to stay ten years instead of two. Getting rid of a problem and creating enthusiasm are two different projects, using two different tools, and no amount of roof repair will ever accidentally produce enthusiasm.

The business version of this is the trap almost every organisation falls into at least once. A company notices morale sagging and responds the way most companies respond, with a salary revision. For about a month, complaints genuinely drop, and everyone in the leadership meeting congratulates themselves on having solved engagement. Then, six or eight weeks later, engagement scores quietly slide right back to where they started, and the same leadership team is baffled, because they just spent real money on the problem. What actually happened is entirely predictable under Herzberg. The raise is hygiene. It removed a grievance. It was never capable of manufacturing the meaning, recognition, and growth that motivation actually runs on, because those live on a completely different list, and nobody touched that list.

When do you reach for this framework specifically. Whenever you notice a company, or a manager, repeatedly spending the motivator budget on hygiene problems, and getting confused about why engagement will not move. If people are actively complaining, hygiene is probably the honest first fix, and you should make it, because misery is real and worth removing on its own terms. But if people are merely quiet, unremarkable, coasting, going through the motions without complaint but also without any spark, adding more hygiene will not touch that. You need a motivator: give someone a project with their name attached to it, recognise specific work in front of peers, hand over a piece of real responsibility they did not have last month.

So remember: hygiene factors stop the bleeding, motivators build the muscle, and they are not the same list, so check which one you are actually spending money on before you wonder why nothing changed.

Our third puzzle explains one of the ugliest, most consistent biases in how we judge each other, and it is called the fundamental attribution error.

In plain words, it says this. When someone else fails, we explain it by pointing at their character. When we ourselves fail, at the very same task, we explain it by pointing at our circumstances. His report was late, so he must be disorganised, or lazy, or careless. My report was late because the client sent the data four days behind schedule, and honestly, anyone would have been late given that. Same outcome, a missed deadline, two entirely different stories, and the difference is not the facts, it is simply whose failure it is.

Think about traffic, because everyone recognises this instantly once you say it out loud. When another driver cuts you off, your immediate, automatic thought is that person is reckless, that person is a bad driver. When you cut someone off, your immediate, automatic thought is I did not see them, the sun was in my eyes, I was running late for something urgent. You have the same piece of evidence, a car swerving into your lane, and you interpret it completely differently depending on who is behind the wheel. That gap between the two explanations is the entire bias, sitting right there in a five second traffic moment.

At work, this bias becomes genuinely expensive during appraisal season, because it quietly, invisibly, distorts the story a manager tells about an underperforming employee. The employee missed a deadline because the vendor was late delivering a critical component, an external, situational fact. But the manager, reviewing the miss from the outside, without having lived through the vendor delay personally, writes in the appraisal notes that the employee shows poor planning and time management. The employee, defending themselves, points at the vendor. The manager, unconsciously protected by the very same bias working in the other direction, is confident the real problem is the employee's character. Neither person is lying. Both are running the identical, entirely normal cognitive shortcut, just from opposite seats.

Use this framework specifically at the exact moment you catch yourself forming a judgement about someone else's failure, particularly right before you write anything down in a performance review or say anything in a one on one. The fix the course material points toward is not a moral instruction to be kinder, because moral instructions do not survive contact with a stressful Monday. The fix is procedural. Before you record any judgement about a person's character, force yourself to write the situational explanation first, in full, as if you were building the strongest possible case for the other side. Then check whether that situational story actually holds up against what you know. More often than you would expect, it does, and the character judgement you were about to write quietly dissolves once the situational one is on the page in front of you.

So remember: we explain their failures with character and our own with circumstance, and the only real defence is writing the circumstantial story down first, before you let yourself write the character one.

Pause here and try this yourself. Think of the last time you were genuinely annoyed at a colleague for something they did, or did not do. Before reading on, try to write, honestly, the strongest possible situational explanation for their behaviour, the one you would offer if it had been you in their chair. Here is how that usually plays out when people actually do it instead of skipping past it. Most people find, uncomfortably, that a perfectly reasonable situational story was available all along, and that they had simply not bothered looking for it, because the character explanation arrived first, felt satisfying, and closed the question before the situational one ever got a chance to compete. That gap, between the explanation that arrives first and the explanation that is actually true, is exactly what the fundamental attribution error lives inside.

Now to the fourth puzzle, and this is the one that opened today's episode, because it explains Sanjay and Meera perfectly.

Researchers Tiziana Casciaro and Miguel Sousa Lobo studied how expertise actually moves, or fails to move, inside real organisations, and their finding earned a memorable, slightly mischievous name: competent jerks and lovable fools. When people need advice or help at work, and they are choosing whom to ask, they are not running a pure search for the best answer. They are running a search that weighs two separate things at once, how good is this person's knowledge, and how much do I enjoy interacting with this person. And here is the uncomfortable part of the finding. Given a real choice, most people will consult the likeable colleague with average knowledge over the highly capable colleague who is difficult to deal with. Liking beats competence in the actual, revealed choice of whom people go and ask.

This produces a strange and costly outcome inside a company. An unlikeable expert's knowledge effectively stops circulating. It sits there, real and valuable, unused, because the informal traffic of questions and favours that actually moves expertise around an organisation routes around that person rather than through them. Meanwhile a well liked colleague with only ordinary knowledge becomes, almost by accident, one of the most consulted people in the building, not because their answers are the best available, but because asking them costs nothing socially and often something pleasant.

An everyday example: think of two shopkeepers on the same street selling roughly the same goods at roughly the same prices. One is brusque, efficient, technically excellent at fixing whatever you bring in, but makes you feel rushed and slightly foolish for asking questions. The other is warmer, chats with you, remembers your last visit, and is honestly a little less skilled at the actual repair. Most people, across enough repeat visits, drift toward the warmer shop, even though they would tell you, if pressed directly, that the other one does better work.

The business version is exactly the Sanjay and Meera story from the top of this episode, and it is worth stating the organisational cost plainly. Casciaro and Lobo's research points to two structural remedies, because telling the competent jerk to simply be nicer rarely survives past the training session. First, proximity: physically or organisationally placing the difficult expert closer to where decisions get made, so people bump into their knowledge without having to actively choose to seek it out. Second, and more powerful, brokers: identifying the well liked, well connected people in a team and deliberately routing them toward the expert, so the lovable colleague becomes the carrier who circulates the difficult expert's knowledge outward to everyone else. The broker gets some of the social credit, the expert's knowledge finally gets used, and everybody wins except the appraisal system, which usually has no line item at all for either of these things, network position and knowledge circulation, and keeps rewarding only the visible individual output it knows how to measure.

Use this framework whenever you notice that good ideas or accurate warnings are not travelling through your organisation despite sitting inside it somewhere. It is also worth using defensively, on yourself, if you suspect you might be the competent jerk in your own team, because the fix there is entirely within your control, and it is not becoming a worse expert, it is becoming a slightly warmer door into the same expertise.

So remember: people consult who they like more reliably than who they rate, an unlikeable expert's knowledge goes quiet, and the fix is proximity and brokers, not a lecture about niceness.

Our final framework for today moves from research studies into two real coaching careers, because it answers a question every leader eventually has to face honestly.

Consider Bobby Knight and Mike Krzyzewski, two of the most successful coaches in American college basketball, and, for a period, teacher and former player working the same broad tradition of discipline and demanding standards. Knight built a program on fear. Extraordinary intensity, explosive confrontation, standards enforced through the sheer force of his presence in the room. It won. Three national championships sit on that record. Krzyzewski, known widely as Coach K, built his program on something that looked similar from a distance, relentlessly high standards, total intolerance for sloppiness, but arrived at differently, through standards the players were taught to hold for themselves rather than standards imposed on them moment to moment by a single dominant figure. Knight was fired from Indiana in two thousand. Krzyzewski went on to sustain success across four decades.

Here is the test that actually separates these two models, and it is simple enough to apply to any leader you have ever worked for, including yourself. Ask what happens to the standard the moment the leader leaves the room. Under a fear based system, the answer is immediate and predictable: the standard leaves with the leader, because it was never internal to the team, it was external, imposed, enforced by presence. The instant enforcement stops, so does the behaviour it was enforcing. Under an internalised system, the standard survives the leader's absence, because it was never actually about the leader in the first place, it had been transferred into the players themselves, and they carry it into the room whether or not anyone is watching.

Everyday version of the same idea: think of two ways a parent gets a child to keep their room tidy. One way is threatening consequences every single time, standing over the child until the room is clean. The room gets clean, reliably, but only while the parent is standing there, and it degrades within a day of the parent's attention moving elsewhere. The other way is slower and harder, helping the child actually feel the difference between a tidy space and a messy one, until keeping it tidy becomes something the child does because it matters to them, not because someone is checking. The second method takes longer to build and survives far longer once built.

In business, this test separates two kinds of turnaround leader you meet constantly in case studies and in real companies. One type walks into a struggling unit, drives extraordinary short term results through sheer personal intensity, hits every number, and then moves on to the next assignment, promoted for the visible turnaround. Eighteen months later, someone notices the unit has quietly slid back to where it started, because the standards never actually became the team's own, they were the leader's, on loan, and the loan was recalled the day the leader left. The other type builds more slowly, sometimes frustratingly so from a head office watching quarterly numbers, but leaves behind a team that keeps performing at the new standard for years after that leader has moved on, promoted or not, present or not, because the standard now genuinely belongs to the people who have to live it every day, not to the person who first demanded it.

Use this framework specifically when you are evaluating a leader's real impact, not their visible one. Do not stop at what the numbers did while they were in the seat. Ask, honestly, what you would predict for those same numbers a year after they leave. That single mental exercise separates fear from internalisation faster than almost anything else available to you.

So remember: fear based standards leave the room with the leader, internalised standards stay behind, and the only honest test of a leader's real work is what survives their absence.

Pause here and try this yourself. Think of a team or classroom you were once part of that had genuinely high standards. Before reading on, ask yourself honestly whether those standards survived the departure of whoever set them, a teacher moving on, a manager transferring, a captain graduating. If the standard collapsed the moment that person left, you were looking at fear, however well it might have worked at the time. If it held, you were looking at something that had been internalised, and it is worth asking what that particular leader actually did, in the small daily moments, that got the standard to move from their head into everyone else's.

Now let us slow down and spend real time inside two full stories, because frameworks only earn their keep once you have watched them play out completely, start to finish.

Return to Sanjay and Meera, because their story does not end with the new hire's messy attribution model. Over the following year, a pattern set in that nobody in leadership had designed and nobody could point to on a single date it started. Junior analysts routed almost everything, questions, second opinions, requests for a sanity check on a tricky number, toward Meera. Sanjay's queue of requests, if you could call it that, thinned out until it was mostly senior people, the ones with enough standing not to worry about the interaction cost of approaching him. The strange part is what this did to actual output quality. Meera, generous with her time and genuinely trying her best, was solving problems at her own level of skill, which was solid but not exceptional. Sanjay's much sharper instincts, the kind that catch a subtle error before it reaches a client six weeks later, were increasingly reserved for the handful of senior colleagues willing to pay the social cost of talking to him, which meant an entire layer of junior work was quietly running at a lower quality ceiling than the building's actual talent could have supported, and almost nobody upstairs could see it, because nothing about it showed up as a missed deadline or a visible error, it showed up only as a slow accumulation of slightly worse decisions that never individually looked like a crisis.

The turn came, as it often does, almost by accident. A new team lead, without knowing any of the research behind it, made one structural change: she started pairing Sanjay with a junior analyst on a rotating basis for a specific technical review each week, not as a favour to Sanjay, but as a formal part of the workflow, so nobody had to choose to approach him, the approach was simply built into the calendar. Within two months, something else shifted. That junior analyst, who happened to be well liked across the floor, started fielding questions from peers about the very things Sanjay had taught her, effectively carrying his knowledge outward in a form the rest of the team found easy to receive. Nobody called it brokering. Nobody had read the paper. But it was, in exact miniature, the structural fix Casciaro and Lobo's research points toward, proximity built into the schedule, and a liked colleague acting as the channel. Sanjay did not get warmer. He did not need to. The organisation simply stopped depending on people choosing to walk over to him, and started routing his knowledge to where it was needed regardless of how approachable he felt on any given Tuesday.

Now the second story, the coaches, told at fuller length. Bobby Knight's teams at Indiana were, by any measure, extraordinary for a long stretch, three national titles across his tenure, built on a system where his presence, his intensity, and often his temper, were the enforcement mechanism for every standard on the floor. Players who came through that program frequently describe it in the same breath as both formative and frightening, a system that produced genuine excellence while it was running, and produced it specifically because Knight was standing there making sure it ran. When Indiana finally dismissed him in two thousand, after years of escalating conflict around his conduct, the program did not merely lose a coach, it lost the enforcement mechanism the entire standard had been built on, because the standard had never fully migrated out of him and into the culture of the team itself.

Krzyzewski, across the river of decades that followed, took a visibly different route to a similar intensity. He was not soft, by any account, his standards for effort and preparation were every bit as unforgiving as Knight's, but the process by which players absorbed those standards ran through something closer to teaching and internal ownership than through fear of a single man's temper. Players who came up under his program describe carrying the standard with them after they had graduated, applying it in professional careers, in coaching jobs of their own, in contexts Krzyzewski was nowhere near to enforce anything. That is the tell. The standard had migrated. It was no longer only his, it belonged, at least in part, to everyone who had passed through the program, which is a large part of why his success sustained across four decades rather than ending the moment any single relationship or contract did.

The lesson these two coaching lives leave behind for anyone managing a team of their own is not that fear never works, because it plainly can, in the short run, and can even win championships while it runs. The lesson is that fear is a rental agreement, not a purchase. You get the behaviour only for as long as you are actively paying the rent of your own presence and intensity. Internalisation is slower to build and far less dramatic to watch from the outside, but it is the only version of a standard you actually get to keep once you have left the room.

Before we close, a short list of the ways today's ideas get misapplied, because half the value of a framework is knowing where people usually get it wrong.

The first trap is treating expectancy theory as an excuse to throw money at every motivation problem. Money only ever fixes the middle term, whether effort gets rewarded. If the real block is the first term, someone genuinely doubting their own capability, or the third term, someone who does not actually want what is on offer, a bigger number in the reward column will not move either of those beliefs, and you will spend real budget for no real change.

The second trap is applying Herzberg as a reason to underpay people while calling it motivation strategy. Hygiene factors are not optional decoration. If pay is genuinely unfair or conditions are genuinely poor, people will disengage regardless of how much meaningful work or recognition you layer on top, because a missing foundation is not something a nicer roof can compensate for.

The third trap, with the attribution error, is assuming awareness alone fixes the bias. Simply knowing this bias exists does not stop it from operating on you at nine in the evening while you are finishing appraisal forms under deadline pressure. It requires an actual procedural habit, writing the situational case first, every single time, rather than relying on the memory that the bias is real in the abstract.

The fourth trap, with competent jerks and lovable fools, is concluding that niceness alone should now outrank competence in every decision. That is not the finding. The finding is a warning about an unconscious bias distorting how expertise actually flows, not a new instruction to promote likeability over ability. The fix is structural, proximity and brokers, not a demand that your best expert become a different personality.

And the final trap, with Knight and Krzyzewski, is assuming intensity itself is the villain of the story. It is not. Both coaches were intense. The variable that actually mattered was where the standard lived, inside the leader alone, or inside the team as well. Do not mistake calm for internalisation, and do not mistake intensity for fear. Check where the standard actually lives, and you will have your real answer.

Let us bring today home with a short revision sprint, because these ideas are most useful when you can recall them in seconds, not minutes.

Motivation is a multiplication of three beliefs, can I, will I be rewarded, do I care, and any single zero collapses the whole product, so find the zero before you reach for a bigger reward. Herzberg splits the world into hygiene, which only removes pain, and motivators, which actually build drive, and mixing up which list you are spending on explains most failed engagement efforts. The fundamental attribution error has us blaming character in others and circumstance in ourselves for the identical failure, and the only real defence is writing the situational story down first, deliberately, before the character story gets to write itself. Competent jerks and lovable fools reminds us that people consult who they like more reliably than who they rate, which means an organisation's best knowledge can sit unused unless proximity and brokers carry it outward. And Knight against Krzyzewski gives you the sharpest test of all for any leader, including yourself: would the standard survive a month of your absence, or does it belong only to you.

Your one sentence for organisational behaviour: find the zero in the multiplication, spend motivators on motivation and hygiene on complaints, write the situational story before the character story, and build standards, and knowledge, so they survive without you standing in the room.
