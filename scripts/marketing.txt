The plumber crouches under the sink for a long moment, then stands up, wipes his hands on a rag, and shakes his head slowly. The homeowner is holding a glossy brochure, the kind with a shower unit photographed like a piece of jewellery, water falling in a perfect silver sheet, a small gold sticker in the corner announcing a design award. She had seen it in a magazine. She had wanted it in her bathroom for three weeks now.

The plumber turns the brochure over in his hands, reads the specification sheet, and hands it back. He tells her, in the gentle but final tone tradesmen use when they have made up their mind, that he would rather fit something he knows. Something with fewer wires near the water. Something that will not have her calling him back in November when it breaks down and the company that made it is impossible to reach on the phone. He has fitted a hundred showers in his life and burned his fingers, so to speak, on exactly two clever ones that promised the world and left him holding an angry customer's complaint. He is not going to make it a third. He recommends the plain mixer tap instead, the one he has fitted a thousand times, the one that never surprises him.

She listens. She trusts him more than she trusts the brochure, because he is standing in her house and the company in the brochure is not. Three streets away, another homeowner is having exactly the same conversation with a different plumber, and the answer is exactly the same.

Now scale that conversation up. Somewhere in a design studio, a team had spent two years building a shower valve that finally solved the oldest complaint in every British bathroom, water that turns scalding or freezing the instant someone flushes a toilet elsewhere in the house. They built it well. They tested it well. Independent judges gave it a design award. And in its first four months on the market, it sold eighty one units. Not eighty one thousand. Eighty one. A brilliant product, walking straight into an invisible wall built entirely out of plumbers' opinions, one crouched conversation under one sink at a time.

Hello, and welcome to the Decision Notebook listening library. Today, marketing. We are going to spend this episode taking apart the idea that marketing means advertising, because it does not, and building up the real one in its place: marketing as the discipline of choosing which customers to serve and then arranging the whole firm, product, price, channel, people, to deserve them. We will work through four frameworks, segmenting and targeting and positioning, the mechanics and dangers of a distribution channel, the way an invisible gatekeeper can stall a great product exactly as we just watched, and the strange arithmetic of service recovery. Along the way we will pause twice for exercises you can work through with me out loud, and we will close with three full case stories and the traps that catch even careful managers.

Let us start with the one thread that ties the whole subject together, because once you see it, you cannot unsee it.

The thread is this. Every marketing decision, whether it is which customer segment to chase, which shop to sell through, which feature to build first, or how to respond when something goes wrong, is really the same decision wearing a different costume. It is the decision of who you are choosing to serve, and what you are choosing to do to earn the right to keep serving them. Segmentation is choosing before you speak. Positioning is deciding what you want to be remembered for, in one sentence, inside a customer's head who has forty other things competing for that same head. Channel strategy is choosing who stands between you and the customer and whether that person or shop has a reason to help you or a reason to quietly work against you. And service recovery is what happens when the choosing and the deserving temporarily break down in public, and you have to prove, in front of a watching customer, that the choice to serve them was real.

Notice what is absent from that thread. Advertising is absent. Advertising is not absent because it does not matter, it matters a great deal, and we will spend the whole next episode on it. It is absent because advertising is downstream. Advertising tells people about a choice that marketing has already made. If the choice underneath is wrong, no advertisement fixes it, it only makes the wrong choice louder and more expensive. A shower company can buy every billboard in the country and it will not open a single plumber's mind if the plumber has already decided, based on a hundred jobs of experience, not to trust it. So before you ever write a single word of a campaign, marketing's real job has already happened or already failed. That is the whole subject in one sentence, and now let us build it up properly, one framework at a time.

Our first framework is segmentation, targeting, and positioning, usually shortened to S T P, and it is the closest thing this course has to a starting ritual, because almost every other marketing decision assumes it has already been done.

In plain words, here is what S T P actually asks you to do. First, segment: accept that your market is not one crowd of identical people, it is several smaller crowds who each want something a little different, and cut it along those real differences rather than convenient ones like age or city. Second, target: look honestly at those smaller crowds and pick the ones you, specifically, given your strengths and your limits, have the best chance of winning, and let go of the rest, at least for now. Third, position: decide the one idea you want to occupy in the target customer's mind, and say it so clearly and so often that it becomes the answer they give when someone asks them what you are for.

Here is an everyday version of it, the kind you have probably watched without naming it. Think of a private tutor setting up shop in a neighbourhood full of school students. She could try to teach every subject to every age group, and she would be a blur, forgettable, competing with every other tutor on price because nobody can tell her apart from the rest. Instead, watch what the successful ones actually do. She looks at the crowd of possible students and sees it is not one crowd. There are curious younger children who need patience and games. There are calm, steady students who mostly need a quiet room to study in. And there are anxious, high-pressure students staring down a board exam in six months, terrified of mathematics specifically. She picks the third group, because she is good at calm explanation under pressure and because that group pays well for peace of mind, not just for knowledge. She becomes, in that neighbourhood, the maths fixer for panicking exam students. Parents do not compare her price to the general tutoring market anymore, because in their heads she is not in that market, she is in a category of one. That is segmentation, targeting, and positioning, done by instinct, by someone who has probably never heard the phrase.

Now let us put spoken numbers on it with a business example, and let us build it carefully so the logic is visible. Imagine a company selling a subscription reading app, choosing where to spend its first year of marketing money. Say the total pool of possible adult subscribers in its target cities is around ten lakh people. Inside that ten lakh, imagine three real segments emerge from research, not from guessing. About four lakh are school and college students, price sensitive, only willing to pay a small amount a month, but numerous. About three lakh are working professionals who read for stress relief on their commute and are willing to pay considerably more for convenience and a clean, fast app. And about three lakh are retired readers with plenty of time, moderate willingness to pay, but a strong preference for large print and simplicity, a segment most competitors ignore because it looks unglamorous.

A company with limited marketing money cannot chase all three groups at once, not well. So it targets. Suppose it picks the working professionals, three lakh people, because its app is fast and clean, exactly what that segment values, and because that segment pays the most per person, which matters when your marketing budget is not large. It has now made a choice, and choosing means also choosing not to. It is not going to try to be everything to the students or the retirees this year. Then it positions: not as a reading app for everyone, but specifically as the ten minute escape for your commute, a single sentence built for a working professional standing on a crowded train with fifteen free minutes. Every piece of writing on the app store page, every notification, every design decision, gets checked against that one sentence. Does this serve the ten minute commute story, or does it dilute it. That discipline, refusing every tempting distraction that does not serve the chosen position, is what makes positioning actually stick instead of blurring into noise within a year.

When should you reach for S T P specifically. Reach for it at the start of anything, a new product, a new market entry, a repositioning after a competitor has moved in on your old ground, or honestly, whenever you notice your own marketing message trying to say five different things to five different kinds of people at once. That symptom, a message that tries to please everyone, is almost always evidence that segmentation and targeting were skipped, and the firm jumped straight to communication.

So remember. Segment the market by what people actually want, not by convenient labels. Target the group you can genuinely win, and let the rest go for now. Position with one sentence, said so often it becomes automatic in the customer's head. Choose before you communicate, always in that order, never the reverse.

Pause here and try this yourself. Here is the exercise. A small chain of neighbourhood gyms is deciding who to build its next branch for. Its city has, broadly, three kinds of potential members: busy young professionals who want a fast, no frills workout before office hours, new parents who want childcare included so they can exercise without arranging a babysitter, and retired residents who want low impact classes and a social atmosphere more than an intense workout. The gym only has money to build one specialised branch this year. Walk through S T P out loud with me. First, have we actually segmented properly, three groups with genuinely different needs, not just three age labels. Yes, notice the needs are different, speed and convenience, versus childcare, versus low impact and social. Second, targeting, which group should they choose. That depends on the gym's own strengths, so let us reason it through. If this gym chain already has strong instructors good at gentle group classes and a large existing membership of older residents nearby, the retired segment is the natural target, because they can win it with what they already have, not what they would have to build from scratch. Third, positioning, what is the one sentence. Something like, the gym that never rushes you. Now check it, does every part of that branch, the class schedule, the music volume, the front desk conversation, serve that one sentence. If a manager quietly plans loud music and thirty minute high intensity classes at the same branch, that is the s t p discipline breaking down in real time, and you should be able to hear it breaking as clearly as I can hear it in this example.

Our second framework moves from choosing the customer to choosing the road that gets your product to them, and it comes with a warning built into its very name: channel conflict.

In plain words, a channel is simply the path a product travels between the maker and the final buyer, a dealer, a shop, a website, a distributor, sometimes several of these stacked one after another. And channel conflict is what happens when you add a new path to reach more customers, and the old path notices, resents it, and starts working against you instead of for you. Here is the law underneath it, and it is worth saying slowly. Every new route to market grows your volume and taxes the old route's loyalty. Always. The only real choice you have is whether you pay that tax deliberately and on your own terms, or pay it by accident, in the form of quiet sabotage you only notice a year later when your oldest partners have started pushing a competitor's product instead of yours.

Here is the everyday version. Picture the neighbourhood electronics repair shop that has fixed your family's appliances for fifteen years, loyally stocking a certain brand of parts because the brand always treated it fairly. Then that same brand opens a flashy showroom two streets away, selling direct to customers at a slightly lower price, cutting the repair shop out entirely for anyone who wants a straightforward replacement. The repair shop owner is not foolish. He notices instantly. And the next time a customer asks him, casually, which brand to buy, he no longer recommends that one. He recommends whichever brand still needs him, still respects him, still lets him earn a living. The company did not lose him through some grand betrayal. It lost him through simple, rational self-interest, the same self-interest that made him loyal in the first place.

The business example the course leans on hardest is a large American tyre maker and its new wet-grip tyre. The tyre maker had built its business for decades on independent tyre dealers, loyal partners who carried the brand, trained their staff on it, and by themselves accounted for roughly half of the tyre maker's total revenue, spread across around four thousand four hundred dealer locations. Then large mass merchant retailers came along, offering enormous volume, shelf space in stores millions of people already walked through every week. The tyre maker, tempted by that volume, put its tyres on those mass merchant shelves too. And exactly as the law predicts, two things happened at once. Volume went up, because now its tyres were available in places dealers could never match. And the dealers, the ones responsible for roughly half the company's revenue and decades of loyalty, felt the ground shift under them and were furious. Many quietly began steering customers toward other brands, brands that had not just competed with them out of the other side of the same company's mouth.

Notice, this was not a story of a bad decision followed by ruin. Mass volume growth is genuinely valuable, and no serious retailer today can afford to ignore large retail channels entirely. The mistake, if there was one, was treating the channel addition as a pure win rather than a trade with a real cost attached, a cost that needed managing on purpose, through things like protected dealer territories, dealer exclusive product lines the mass channel never carries, or price floors that stop the new channel from simply undercutting the old one on the exact same item. Pay the tax on your own terms, with a plan, or pay it later, in dealer defections you cannot easily reverse.

When should you reach for this framework. Reach for it the moment anyone in the room proposes adding a new sales channel, a marketplace listing, a big box retail deal, a direct to consumer website that competes with your own distributors, and ask out loud, before agreeing, who loses from this, and what do we owe them to keep them from quietly working against us.

So remember. Every new channel that grows your reach also taxes the loyalty of the channel you already have. You cannot avoid paying that tax. You can only choose whether you pay it deliberately, with protection built in for your original partners, or pay it later, in the form of defections and lost trust you did not see coming.

Pause here and try this yourself. Here is the second exercise, and let us do the arithmetic properly this time, using illustrative figures built to make the logic visible, not disclosed figures from any real company. Suppose a mid-sized furniture brand earns two crore rupees a year in total sales. One crore, exactly half, comes through sixty independent showroom partners who have carried the brand for years. The company is considering listing its full catalogue on a large online marketplace, which its own research suggests could add forty lakh rupees of entirely new sales in year one, customers the showrooms were never going to reach anyway, people in smaller towns with no showroom nearby. Sounds like a clean win, forty lakh rupees of new revenue with no downside. But now apply the law. Suppose, based on what happened to comparable brands, around fifteen percent of the existing one crore rupees of showroom sales quietly shifts onto the marketplace too, because some showroom customers, once they see the same product online at a slightly lower price with free delivery, simply buy there instead. Fifteen percent of one crore rupees is fifteen lakh rupees. So the real net gain is not forty lakh rupees, it is forty lakh minus fifteen lakh, twenty five lakh rupees, and that twenty five lakh rupees needs to be weighed against the resentment of sixty showroom partners who now see their own product cheaper online than they are permitted to sell it for. The honest business case is twenty five lakh rupees of net new revenue, plus a real relationship cost, not forty lakh rupees of free money. That difference, between the number that sounds good in a slide and the number left after you subtract the channel's tax, is exactly the discipline this framework is asking you to practise every single time.

Our third framework returns us to where we began this episode, the shower under the plumber's disapproving hands, and it teaches something that catches brilliant products off guard again and again: the gatekeeper problem, sitting inside the wider idea of how new things actually spread through a market.

In plain words, here is the trap. We like to imagine that a customer decides to buy something after weighing its features against their needs, alone, rationally, directly. But for a great many products, especially ones that are complicated, expensive, or installed by somebody else, the real buyer never gets a clean, unfiltered look at the product at all. Between the company and the final customer sits a gatekeeper, someone the customer trusts more than they trust an advertisement, and that gatekeeper's fear, not the customer's actual need, becomes the real obstacle. Fix the gatekeeper's fear, or the market never even hears about your product's yes, no matter how loudly you say it yourself.

The everyday version is the used car sale. You do not trust the seller's word about the engine, not fully, however honest he sounds. You take the car to a mechanic you already trust, and whatever that mechanic says in five minutes, walking around the car with a torch, overrides every sentence the seller has said in the last hour. The mechanic is the gatekeeper. The seller's product could be flawless, and it will not matter if the mechanic, out of caution, out of a bad past experience with that make of car, out of simple unfamiliarity, shakes his head.

The business example is a British shower maker and its new digital shower, the story we opened with, and it deserves its full shape now. A British company engineered a genuinely clever product, a shower valve that solved the classic complaint of scalding or freezing water whenever someone else in the house used a tap. It won design awards. Independent judges, looking purely at engineering and design, loved it. And in its first four months on sale, it sold eighty one units. Why did a better product, recognised as better by experts, sell so little. Because homeowners in Britain do not typically install their own showers, plumbers do, and plumbers had been burned before, more than once, by clever electronic showers that broke down, that were fiddly to install, that generated angry callback complaints months later with no easy fix. So when a homeowner asked her plumber about this new award winning shower, the plumber, exactly like the one in our opening story, quietly steered her toward something familiar instead. The gatekeeper's caution, built from real past pain, was the actual bottleneck, not the customer's interest and not the product's quality. The shower maker eventually broke through, not by advertising harder to homeowners, but by going directly at the gatekeeper's fear, putting the product physically into plumbers' hands, letting them install it once, feel how straightforward it actually was, and convert through direct experience rather than a brochure.

When should you reach for this framework. Reach for it whenever your product or service passes through a professional intermediary before it reaches the end user, a doctor prescribing, an architect specifying, a plumber installing, a financial adviser recommending, a distributor deciding what to stock. Whenever sales are disappointing despite genuine product quality, ask first whether the real customer even heard your pitch, or whether a cautious gatekeeper quietly filtered it out before it arrived.

So remember. When your product flows through a sceptical middleman, their risk is your bottleneck, not the end customer's interest. Diagnose the gatekeeper's fear specifically, and solve that fear directly, often through hands-on trial, before you spend another rupee trying to persuade the customer who never actually got the full pitch in the first place.

Pause here and try this yourself. Here is the third exercise, again with illustrative numbers built for practice. Imagine a company making a new kind of home water filter, technically superior, but sold mostly through independent plumbers who recommend a filter whenever they are called in to fix something else in the house. Suppose the company calculates that plumbers visit around two thousand relevant households a month across its target city. If plumbers freely recommended the new filter whenever it was a good fit, the company estimates around thirty percent of those visits, six hundred households, would be a genuine fit for the product. But in practice, this year, only forty of those six hundred good fit households actually get the recommendation, because most plumbers, unfamiliar with the new filter, default to the brand they already trust. That is a conversion of the gatekeeper's own recommending behaviour of under seven percent, forty out of six hundred, even though the underlying customer fit was thirty percent of all visits. Now, if the company runs a programme giving two hundred plumbers a free filter to install in their own homes so they can experience it directly, and that direct trial lifts their recommendation rate from under seven percent to, say, forty percent of good fit households, the company is not adding a single new customer lead. It is releasing demand that was already there, sitting behind the gatekeeper's caution the whole time. That is the arithmetic version of what happened at the shower maker, and it is why the fix was never a bigger advertisement, it was a screwdriver in a plumber's own hand.

Our fourth and final framework moves from winning the customer to keeping them after something has already gone wrong, and it holds one of the strangest, most counterintuitive findings in the whole subject: the service recovery paradox.

In plain words, here is the puzzle. Common sense says a company with zero failures should have the happiest customers, and a company that fails and then fixes it should logically end up a little worse off than the company that never failed at all. But the actual finding, observed again and again in service settings, is that a failure handled fast, personally, and generously can sometimes leave a customer more loyal than if nothing had gone wrong in the first place. The reason is that a smooth, uneventful stay never gives the customer any evidence about how the company behaves under pressure. A handled failure does. It proves, in a way ordinary smoothness cannot, that someone was actually paying attention.

The everyday version, one you have almost certainly lived through, is a good restaurant getting your order wrong. The kitchen sends the wrong dish. A mediocre restaurant shrugs, maybe apologises flatly, and moves on. A genuinely good one notices instantly, apologises with real attention, remakes the dish fast, and often removes it from the bill without being asked. You often remember that meal more warmly than the ten meals that went perfectly, because the mistake became a moment where you actually got to see the restaurant's character, and it turned out to be a good one.

The business example is a luxury hotel chain, set at a premium resort, the kind where a guest's total experience is the entire product being sold, not just the room. A guest complains, specifically, about a train whistle audible from the room at night, a small thing, arguably outside the hotel's control entirely. The hotel does not dismiss it as unfixable. Staff locate the source of the complaint, move quickly to address what can genuinely be addressed within the hour, whether that means adjusting the room assignment or another concrete step, and then follow up with the guest personally, not through a form letter but through a real person checking in. That combination, speed and personal attention, is what converts a complaint into loyalty rather than resentment. The guest leaves having learned something an untroubled stay never would have taught them, that when something goes wrong at this resort, somebody notices and somebody acts.

But here is the discipline the case demands, and it is easy to miss if you only remember the pleasant part of the finding. The effect works once. It works for first failures, genuinely surprising ones, handled fast. It is not a licence to fail repeatedly and recover repeatedly, hoping for the same warm effect each time, because a second and third failure reads not as attentiveness but as incompetence wearing a smile. And it only works if the response is fast. A recovery that takes three days to even acknowledge the complaint has already lost the moment; slowness converts the same complaint from an opportunity into confirmation that nobody was, in fact, paying attention.

When should you reach for this framework. Reach for it the instant something goes wrong in front of a customer, because the clock starts the moment they notice the failure, not the moment you decide how to respond. And reach for it structurally too, when designing service standards, by building genuine authority for frontline staff to fix problems on the spot, because a recovery that has to be escalated up three levels of approval has usually lost its speed before it ever reaches the customer.

So remember. A failure handled fast, personally, and generously can build more loyalty than no failure at all, because it proves attentiveness that a smooth stay cannot prove. But the effect is fragile, it works for first failures only, it depends entirely on speed, and it is never, under any circumstance, a reason to relax on preventing failures in the first place.

Let us now sit inside these three stories a little longer, because the frameworks only really live once you have watched them play out in full.

Return once more to the shower maker, because the ending matters as much as the beginning. The company had built something genuinely better, engineers proud of it, judges awarding it, and for four straight months the market simply refused to notice, eighty one units sold against a factory capable of producing vastly more. Inside the company, the natural instinct after a launch like that is panic aimed in the wrong direction, more advertising, a bigger campaign, louder claims about the award it had won. None of that would have worked, because none of that reached the actual decision maker, the plumber standing in someone's bathroom with a torch and fifteen years of hard won caution. The turn came only when the company stopped talking past the plumbers and started talking to them, putting the product directly into their hands, in their own homes, letting them discover through their own fingers that installation was not the nightmare they feared. Trial, not persuasion, was the medicine, because the plumbers' scepticism had never been an opinion open to argument, it was a scar from past products, and scars do not respond to brochures, they respond to new, better evidence gathered firsthand. Once enough plumbers had installed one themselves and found it painless, they began recommending it the way the mechanic recommends a car he has personally checked, and the eighty one units began to look like a slow, difficult, but genuinely necessary first chapter rather than a failure.

Return to the tyre maker, and notice what actually happened once the dealers' anger became visible. This was not a company that had done something reckless out of ignorance, mass retail was too large an opportunity to responsibly ignore forever, and the extra volume it brought was real. The tension in the story is the tension every growing company eventually meets, the pull between reaching new customers today and protecting the loyalty that built the business in the first place. Some of that tension never fully resolves, and that is the honest lesson, not a tidy one. What separates a company that manages it well from one that does not is whether the dealers were treated as a cost to be paid on purpose, with real efforts at protection, distinct product lines, price floors, communication before the mass retail deal rather than after, or as an afterthought, informed only once the shelves were already stocked and the betrayal already felt. The resolution in cases like this is rarely a clean victory. It is a company learning, sometimes expensively, that loyalty which took decades to build can be damaged in a single unguarded quarter, and that the guarding has to be deliberate, structural, and continuous, not a one time apology after the fact.

And return to the hotel chain's resort, where the tension is smaller in scale but sharper in its lesson, because it happens in a matter of hours rather than years. A guest arrives expecting the total, curated calm that a premium resort sells as its entire reason for existing, and a train whistle threatens to break that spell, an inconvenience the hotel did not create and could not, strictly speaking, be blamed for. The easy failure mode here is defensiveness, explaining to the guest, accurately but uselessly, that the railway line predates the hotel and there is nothing to be done. The resolution the case actually describes is different in character entirely. Staff treat the complaint as real regardless of whose fault it technically is, move within the hour to address what genuinely can be addressed, and follow up personally rather than administratively. The guest's experience of the resort is not damaged by the whistle, it is, if anything, deepened by watching the hotel take a small, awkward, partly unfixable problem seriously and fast. That is the entire mechanism of the service recovery paradox, made visible in one train whistle and one hour of attentive staff response.

Before we close, let us name the traps that catch even careful, experienced managers in this subject, because knowing a framework and applying it correctly under pressure are two different skills.

The first trap is confusing what you sell with what the customer actually buys. Nobody wakes up wanting a drill, they want a hole in the wall, and if a competitor offers an easier way to make that hole, your beautifully engineered drill becomes irrelevant overnight, however proud you are of it. Nobody buys premium coffee purely for caffeine, many are buying a comfortable place to sit for an hour, a third space between home and office, and a shop that sells excellent coffee but offers nowhere pleasant to sit has misunderstood its own product entirely. Always ask what job the customer is actually hiring your product to do, and check that your positioning speaks to that job, not to the feature list.

The second trap is treating distribution and brand as if they were fast, flexible assets you can rebuild quickly if a decision turns out wrong. They are the opposite. They are the slowest things a company builds and, cruelly, among the fastest things a company can damage, sometimes with a single short term decision that looked harmless in isolation. A discount that trains loyal customers to always wait for a sale poisons full price demand for years, not weeks. A channel decision made purely for a quarterly volume target can cost you a distributor relationship it took a decade to earn. The discipline this demands is simple to state and hard to practise, check every short term promotional or channel decision against the question of what it silently teaches the customer or the partner to expect from you going forward, because that expectation, once set, is remarkably difficult to unset.

The third trap, related to both, is mistaking a segmentation exercise for market research theatre rather than a real commitment. Many firms produce a beautiful slide with four or five customer personas, complete with names and photographs, and then proceed to market to all of them identically anyway, because true targeting means genuinely walking away from some segments, and that feels uncomfortable in a room full of people who all want to believe their product is for everyone. A segmentation exercise that changes nothing about where the marketing money actually goes was never really segmentation, it was decoration.

Let us bring this home with a quick revision sprint before we close. Marketing begins with choosing, not communicating, segment the market honestly, target where you can genuinely win, position with one sentence said until it becomes automatic. Every channel you add grows reach and taxes the loyalty of the channel you already have, so pay that tax on purpose or pay it later in defections you did not see coming. When your product flows through a cautious gatekeeper, a plumber, a doctor, a distributor, their fear is the real obstacle, and hands on trial usually beats another round of persuasion. And when something breaks in front of a customer, speed and personal attention can turn the failure into proof that you were paying attention all along, though that grace only extends to first failures handled fast, never to a pattern of them.

Your one sentence for marketing: choose your customer before your message, respect the gatekeepers standing inside your channel, and treat every failure as a chance to prove, quickly and personally, that you were listening.
