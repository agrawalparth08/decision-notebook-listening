I want you to imagine a boardroom in Delhi, the first week of November 2006. A phone has just rung. On the line is an investment banker from Rothschild, and the message is short. A much larger European lighting business is for sale. Another bidder is circling. An Indian electricals company has until next Wednesday to put in a bid, or the company goes to somebody else.

Now look at the people in that room. The founder started in 1958 trading electrical goods. His joint managing director is sitting beside him. Their company has just closed the year with sales of about 11,151 million rupees, roughly 249 million dollars. The company on the phone has sales of 473 million euros, roughly 624 million dollars. That is two and a half times their size, and the case itself says approximately twice. It has eleven plants in five countries. It has twenty-two distribution centres. It was in bankruptcy not long ago. It still lost money last year. And the price will be above 200 million dollars, about five times everything the electricals company owns in equity.

Somebody at the table says, we are a company that goes a lot on instinct. Once we enter something, we make it work. Somebody else says, but can we handle the people? That should be our paramount question.

Notice what is not being asked. Nobody is asking whether the electricals company can make a better switch. They already do that well. The question is whether the electricals company should be in a different business at all, in a different continent, and whether the electricals company, as a parent, can make that business worth more than it is worth today.

That is corporate strategy.

Let us begin.

Here is the one thread. If you remember nothing else from the course, remember this thread, because every framework we will meet today is a way of pulling on it.

Business strategy asks how a firm wins in one market. Corporate strategy asks a different question. It asks where the firm should compete at all, and what the corporate parent adds to the businesses and activities it chooses to hold. Where to compete, and what the centre contributes. Two questions. The second one is the uncomfortable one.

See, the head office does not make a single switch, does not weave a single rug, does not operate on a single eye. The businesses do all of that. So the centre must justify itself in some other way. It has to make those businesses better than they would be on their own. If it does, the centre creates value. If it does not, the centre costs money, takes attention, and slows decisions. Then the centre destroys value, and a shareholder would be better off holding the businesses separately.

Now here is the thing. The course never asks you to admire growth. It asks you to test it. Every framework in the guide is a test in disguise. Is this a pocket where we earn more than the going rate? Is this activity in the right country? Is this new business one where our assets actually transfer? Is the premium we are paying smaller than the synergy we can deliver? Can our organisation, with its people and pay systems and habits, actually run what we are buying? Each of those questions is the same question wearing a different coat. Does the centre create value here, or destroy it?

I want you to hold on to that, because the exam will not announce which framework to use. A case will arrive, a founder with a deadline, a plant with four candidate cities, a family firm with two product lines, and you will need to decide which test fits. If you remember the thread, you will always know what you are testing. And the answer will always have to say, in plain words, what the parent adds.

There is also a sequence hidden in the course, which the guide draws as a single line. Start with advantage, the gap between value and cost. Then decide whether to go global. Then decide where each activity should be located. Then decide what to diversify into, and how. And finally ask whether the organisation can execute. Advantage, geography, location, diversification, execution. We will follow that line.

Now the frameworks. Let us take them one at a time, and I will give you the plain words, an everyday picture, a business example, when to reach for it, and a recap.

The first idea is the mini-monopoly. The course starts with perfect competition. Many firms, identical products, everybody knows everybody's price. In that world price is pushed down until marginal revenue equals marginal cost, and profit is zero. Anyone who charges more loses every customer. So the manager's job, the course says, is to escape. Build a pocket where you face less than perfect competition. A mini-monopoly.

Think of the vegetable seller outside your housing society. If five carts sell identical tomatoes at the same corner, nobody earns anything. Now one cart is open at six in the morning, when nobody else is. Or one cart sells only what the residents' WhatsApp group has pre-ordered. That cart has a small pocket of its own. It has earned a little distance from price.

The guide names three levers. First-mover advantage, which means being there before rivals, through patents, brand recall or customer lock-in. Product differentiation, which means making the product meaningfully different. And geography, which means operating where cost is low and market access is high, where rivals lack the edge. Remember that third one. It becomes the whole chipmaker story.

Next, value and cost. The guide gives you three gaps. Value minus price is what the customer keeps. Price minus cost is what the firm keeps. And value minus cost is competitive advantage. The wider that last gap, the more customers you attract. There are only two ways to widen it: raise value, or lower cost. Customers perceive value in four ways, functional, social, experience and financial.

Then the frontier. Draw a curve with non-price value on one axis and relative cost position on the other. Firms on the curve are doing the best that current technology allows. A firm inside the curve has an execution problem, waste and poor process, and the fix is operational efficiency. But that is necessary, not sufficient, because rivals copy best practice. Once you are on the curve, you choose a spot on it, high value at high cost, or low cost at lower value. That choice is strategy. The chipmaker was rare. It sat at high value and low cost together, with faster chips, an ingredient brand, over 80 percent of the CPU market, and a manufacturing scale that let it cut price and keep its margin.

When do you reach for this? At the very start of any case. Ask where the firm earns its gap, and whether the move under discussion widens it.

So remember: perfect competition gives zero profit, strategy builds a mini-monopoly, and the gap between value and cost is the only thing the firm owns.

The second framework is about going global, and it comes from Bartlett and Ghoshal. Going abroad has benefits. New markets, spread of risk, and location economies, which means doing each activity where it is cheapest or best. The guide lists four sources of those economies: cheap resources, accounting arbitrage, government incentives, and a business ecosystem of suppliers and skills. Going abroad also has costs, and they come as two pressures.

Pressure for cost reduction is strong where customers compare on price, as in commodities. Pressure for local responsiveness is strong where tastes and rules differ, as in food, fashion and furniture. Put them on a two by two. High cost pressure with low local pressure is global standardisation, one product everywhere, made in a few cheap places. The guide puts medicine, chemicals and commodities here, and places the chipmaker here too. Low cost pressure with high local pressure is localisation, as in packaged food. Both high is transnational, the hardest to run, because you need global efficiency and local fit at once. Both low is the international model, where a home capability is simply copied abroad.

An everyday picture. A tiffin service in your city and a bottled water brand. The tiffin has to match local taste, street by street. The water is the same everywhere, so the fight is on cost.

Let us try this. The guide gives a caselet of an Indian packaged-spice company wanting to enter the United Kingdom and the Gulf. Recipes and pack sizes must suit each diaspora, and spice sourcing is cheapest in India. The pressure for local responsiveness is high. The cost pressure is moderate. That points to localisation, moving toward transnational as scale grows. Sourcing and grinding stay in India, because they are tied to a resource. Blending and packing can sit near each market. Brand and recipe research stay at home with local input. And watch the downside, duplicated packing cost.

Reach for this when a case asks whether to standardise or adapt. So remember: two pressures decide the shape, cost on one side, local fit on the other, and each activity can sit in a different place.

The third framework is Porter and Rivkin, in their article called Choosing the United States. The big idea is that a location decision is a referendum on a nation's competitiveness. It says sort your activities into three kinds. Resource-tied activities must sit next to a scarce resource, like mining or plantations. Customer-tied activities go where the large, growing, profitable markets are, like direct sales and delivery. And mobile activities can be placed anywhere and moved, like research, software, and goods with modest transport costs.

Mobile activities are the battleground. And here the article makes a sharp point about how managers choose. They use rules of thumb, follow the customer, chase wage rates. They work from poor data, and the guide notes one firm that needed two months just to extract factory cost data. Moves happen a few jobs at a time, like a bucket with pinpricks. And offshoring becomes a fashion, with an industry selling it.

Then come the hidden costs. Direct costs like lower productivity, more scrap and lower first-pass quality. Indirect costs like supervision, training, security, freight and travel. Responsiveness costs, with long lead times. Change over time, and the guide notes Shanghai wages rose about 125 percent between 2006 and 2011. Intellectual property risk. And coordination costs when manufacturing sits far from research. One survey in the reading found that 56 percent of firms moving production offshore saw their total landed cost rise.

The article also says, improve, do not only move. Corning develops its products near its research centre in a town of 11,000, files about 250 patents a year, and then rolls out to 60 plants in 14 countries. And it warns about the subsidy trap. A CEO is quoted saying that if a place has to pay you to come, there is probably a reason, and he considers that a danger sign.

Everyday picture. Choosing a home because the rent is cheap, and then spending three hours a day on the commute. The rent was visible. The commute was hidden.

Reach for this whenever a case asks where an activity should sit. So remember: sort the activity as resource-tied, customer-tied or mobile, then count the hidden costs, because the cheap site is rarely as cheap as the spreadsheet says.

Now we move to diversification, which is the heart of the course. The first cut is related versus unrelated. In related diversification the new business shares resources, skills, technology, channels or customers with the old one. The value comes from economies of scope and from transferring a core competence. In unrelated diversification, the link is only capital and top management. The value is an internal capital market and spreading risk, and the main risk is that shareholders could diversify more cheaply by themselves.

The guide then makes a useful split. Relatedness is not one thing. Market relatedness means same customers, channels and brand. Technology relatedness means same know-how and processes. And a third type is vertical, backward or forward. The electricals company and the lighting business score high on market relatedness. The case says electricals and lighting are sold through common channels, and often bundled in large project bids. But they score low on technology. The case says there is little commonality in the underlying technologies. The eye hospital making its own lenses is vertical.

An everyday picture. A good dosa restaurant adds a sweets counter. The same customers walk in, so the market is related. But the skill of making sweets is different from making dosas.

Reach for this as the first sorting step. So remember: relatedness has types, and market relatedness without technology relatedness gives you a revenue story and a knowledge risk.

The next framework is synergy. The formula is simple. Synergy equals the value of A and B together, minus the value of A plus the value of B separately. And the rule for a premium is simple too. You may pay a premium only if the premium is smaller than the synergy. That is the better-off test in arithmetic form. Does the combination make the whole worth more than the parts, by more than you paid to combine them?

The guide lists the types. Operational synergy, from shared plants, purchasing and logistics. In the electricals deal, moving some lighting production toward low-cost Indian plants. Revenue synergy, from cross-selling through shared channels, like selling the electricals company's switchgear through the lighting business's twenty-two distribution centres. Financial synergy, from lower cost of capital, which was weak here because the lighting business had thin equity, only 18 million euros, and a pension liability of 35 million euros. Managerial synergy, from transferring skills. The electricals company's lean cost culture into the lighting business, and the lighting business's brand know-how back into the electricals company. And then negative synergy, which is integration cost, culture clash and distraction.

The deeper test comes from the resource-based view. Advantage comes from resources that are valuable, rare, inimitable and non-substitutable. If your claimed synergy rests on something any rival could copy, it is not synergy. It is a hope.

Everyday picture. Two neighbouring shops decide to share one rented warehouse. Real saving. Two friends decide to merge their WhatsApp groups. No saving at all.

Reach for this the moment a price or a premium appears. So remember: pay only when the premium is smaller than the synergy, and ask who is actually going to deliver that synergy.

Now the framework the whole course leans on. Markides, in his article To Diversify or Not to Diversify, published in the Harvard Business Review in 1997. His opening line is the one to carry. Before diversifying, think not about what your company does, but about what it does better than its competitors. He notes that most managers tackle the decision with financial analysis, and that is necessary but not sufficient. Then he gives six questions.

Question one. What can our company do better than any competitor in its current market? Define the business by strategic assets, not by its product label. Blue Circle called itself home building related and ended up in bricks, bathtubs and lawn mowers, and failed. Boddington sold its two-hundred-year-old brewery and used its retailing and hospitality skills to move into resort hotels, nursing homes and health clubs, creating enormous shareholder value.

Question two. What strategic assets do we need to succeed in the new market? You need all of them. Coca-Cola had about 90 percent of what wine needed, consumer knowledge, marketing, distribution, but it lacked wine-making, and failed.

Question three. Can we catch up to or leapfrog competitors at their own game? There are three routes. Buy, as Disney did through ties with McDonald's, Mattel and the Florida government. Build, as Sharp did with a 21 million dollar integrated circuit factory in 1969, a quarter of its equity. Or rewrite the rules, as Canon did against Xerox, with small firms as customers, dealers instead of a direct sales force, selling instead of leasing, and quality instead of speed.

Question four. Will diversification break up strategic assets that need to be kept together? McDonald's real estate only works with its quality and its outlets, and Markides' line is that you cannot take the engine out of an airplane and expect it to fly.

Question five. Will we be simply a player in the new market, or will we emerge a winner? This is the acid test. Is the asset rare? Laker Airways failed when British Airways matched it. Is it imitable? A 3M-style culture is not easily copied. Is it substitutable? Dell replaced IBM's dealers. Kao's surface technology won in detergent, with 91 patents and a share rising from 30 to 56 percent, but it was old news in floppy disks.

Question six. What can we learn by diversifying, and are we organised to learn it? Canon's copiers taught it electrostatics, which fed laser printers, and printers taught it electronics, which improved copiers.

Everyday picture. You are very good at cooking for twenty guests. A friend suggests you open a catering business. Fine, that is your asset. A second friend suggests you open a restaurant. Different asset, a location, a daily menu, a kitchen team. Same cooking, different business.

Pause here and try this yourself: a large Indian two-wheeler maker, with 6,000 rural dealers, wants to enter tractors. It has engine know-how and rural finance tie-ups, but no hydraulics or farm-implement skills. Run the six questions and give a go or no-go.

Here is how I would talk it through. Question one, the strategic assets are the rural dealer network, rural finance ties and engine scale. Not the word mobility. Question two, tractors need hydraulics, implements and agronomy service, and it lacks them, so not all assets are present. Question three, buy a small tractor firm, build a hydraulics team, or rewrite the rules with compact tractors for small farms, sold through the existing dealers. Question four, the dealers work because of two-wheeler volume and spares, so tractor service must be built as a separate layer. Question five, rural reach is hard to copy, but large tractor firms also have it, so winning needs a niche, compact and financed. Question six, learning in hydraulics and farm finance could feed rural electric vehicles and utility vehicles. So the verdict is a conditional go, through an alliance or a small acquisition for hydraulics, in the compact niche only, in a separate unit. For full-line tractors, no-go.

Reach for the six questions whenever a firm is stepping into a new market. So remember: assets, not labels; all of them, not most of them; and a winner needs something rare that rivals cannot copy.

Next, the guide's risk check, which the class remembers as MDKFC. It is a five-letter prompt. M for management, do we have the bandwidth to run and integrate this? D for diversification, how far is the new business from our core, in product, market and geography? K for knowledge, do we have the technical and market know-how? C for culture, will values, style and people fit? F for financial, can we fund this without risking the core? The warning signs are practical. A small top team and no M and A function. New product and new market and new country all at once. A key asset missing. Different decision styles. Heavy borrowing or overpaying.

Everyday picture. Before you buy a second flat, you ask yourself five things. Can I manage a tenant on top of my job? Is it far from where I live? Do I know that locality? Will my family agree? Can I pay the loan if the rent stops?

Then comes the entry-mode two by two. One axis is market potential, the other is market uncertainty. High potential with low uncertainty means acquisition, because the value is predictable, so paying a premium for speed makes sense. High potential with high uncertainty means an alliance or joint venture, because you should not pay for certainty you do not have. Share the risk, learn, and keep an option to buy later. Low potential with low uncertainty means grow organically, or do nothing. Low potential with high uncertainty means avoid. And there is a risk-reward ratio behind it. Go only if the expected reward divided by the risk exposure beats your hurdle. If it does not, doing nothing is a valid choice. You can also improve the ratio by staging the commitment, taking a minority stake first, or capping downside with earn-outs.

Pause here and try this yourself: an Indian software firm sees two chances. One, a profitable United States competitor with stable customers is for sale. Two, a fast-growing but unproven artificial intelligence agents market in Southeast Asia, where a local partner offers a joint venture. Place each on the two by two, and run MDKFC on the first.

Talking it through. The first has high potential and low uncertainty, so acquisition is the textbook fit. The second has high potential and high uncertainty, so a joint venture with an option to buy out later. Now MDKFC on the acquisition. Management, can our team run a United States business, and can we retain its leaders? Diversification, same product, new geography, so moderate. Knowledge, we know the product, but we lack United States enterprise sales. Culture, an Indian founder-led firm meeting a United States sales culture, so plan the integration. Financial, check the premium against revenue and cost synergy, and use earn-outs.

So remember: MDKFC finds the risks, the two by two picks the mode, and the risk-reward ratio tells you whether to go at all.

The last framework is the seven S's, from Waterman's article on strategic fit. Its starting idea is that a good strategy is not the same as a doable one. The article quotes an estimate that 90 percent of United States firms fail to develop and execute meaningful strategies. The seven S's are strategy, structure, systems, style, staff, skills and shared values. Structure is the organisation chart. Systems are the processes, like budgeting, quality and performance measurement. Style is what management shows it cares about, by how it spends time and attention, not by what it says. Staff is the people. Skills are what the organisation as a whole can do, which is more than the sum of its people. Shared values are the superordinate goals most people hold. All seven are linked, and none sits above another.

Think of them as compasses. When the needles align, the company is organised. When they do not, it is not, even if the structure looks right.

Everyday picture. A school that announces project-based learning, but still grades only on a final written paper. The strategy says one thing, the system rewards another. The system wins every time.

Pause here and try this yourself: a family-run Indian hospital chain decides to become a digital-first, app-based clinic network. Doctors are paid per consultation, decisions go to the founder, and there are few IT staff. Which S's block the strategy, and what do you change first?

Talking it through. Systems first, because per-consultation pay rewards in-person visits, so change incentives to include tele-consults. Style, because the founder decides everything, and app iteration needs fast local decisions. Staff and skills, because few IT and product people exist, so hire a product team. Structure, create a separate digital unit. Shared values, care in person must widen to care anywhere. Start with systems and structure, because they are cheapest to change and send the biggest signal.

Reach for the seven S's at the end of any case, after the strategy looks good on paper. So remember: the question is not whether the strategy is clever, but whether all seven needles point the same way.

Now let us live inside the cases. Four stories, each with a deadline or a dilemma.

The first is the chipmaker, March 2005. Its general manager of assembly and test has to decide where to build a new plant. Think about what that means. The chipmaker holds over 80 percent of the CPU market. Its customers, Dell at 19 percent of 2004 sales and HP at 16 percent, are moving their business toward Asia. The chipmaker's own sales to Asia-Pacific rose from 26 percent in 2000 to 50 percent in 2004. The chipmaker has a rule that no plant may exceed 40 percent of revenue, and the data gathering behind this decision began in the fall of 2001. So three or four years of research has already been spent.

Here is the first insight, and it is a corporate strategy insight. A wafer fab and an assembly and test plant are two completely different activities. A fab costs 3.5 to 4 billion dollars in the United States, and about a billion a year to run. It needs highly skilled engineers, and it is the most IP-sensitive part of the business, so it stays in the United States, Israel and Ireland. An assembly and test plant costs about 80 million dollars to build, and 150 to 300 million dollars a year to run. It needs 4,000 to 5,000 workers, a plant of 500,000 square feet, twice the size of any existing one, and about fourteen years to reach full operation. Fabs gain from scale. Assembly and test gains from learning, not scale. So it belongs near the customers, where labour is cheap and young and trainable. Locate by activity, not by company.

The screening begins. The chipmaker has tolerances. No voltage sags. Water of about a million gallons a day. Power capacity of at least 40 megavolt-amperes. Four port cities survive. Dalian in China, Chennai in India, Bangkok in Thailand, and Ho Chi Minh City in Vietnam.

Dalian has the best logistics. A container costs 335 dollars to export and 375 to import, against 864 and 1,244 in Chennai. Foreign investment there was 54.9 billion dollars in 2004. The chipmaker has been in China since 1994. But it already has two plants in China and two in Malaysia. Concentration risk. Chennai has English law, highly educated engineers, and an eager state government, but its power and water are unreliable, and its tariffs and container costs are the highest. Bangkok has good infrastructure and an eight-year tax holiday, but AMD is already there, so poaching raises wages. Ho Chi Minh City has power at 6 cents a kilowatt-hour, a 10 percent tax rate for the life of the project, wages of 60 to 80 dollars a month, and no major rival, so the chipmaker would be the anchor tenant. But corruption is high, infrastructure immature, and leadership talent thin.

Here is where the room splits. If you weight cost and portfolio fit, Ho Chi Minh City leads. If you weight infrastructure and logistics, Dalian leads. And the class's resolution is to separate two jobs. Data screens out the sites that fail the tolerances. Strategy chooses among the survivors, by thinking about portfolio, competitors and the long-term commitment of the government. The class notes add that the chipmaker stages large commitments. They record an announcement of 300 million dollars for a smaller plant, then a rise to a billion dollars after ten months of study showed that 500,000 square feet was more efficient. Those notes come from class, not from the case, and the case itself ends before revealing the site.

The second story is the electricals company and the lighting business. We saw the room at the start. Let us go back to it with more detail. The electricals company was not a stranger to deals. It bought a smaller rival in the late nineties and took 60 percent of another maker. In 2005 it made its first foreign purchase, in Greece, for 10 million euros. Then, in January 2006, it lost a United Kingdom firm to Siemens, by less than 10 million pounds, in a deal of about 100 million pounds. It learned how to bid, and built a tie with Deutsche Bank.

Now the tension. The lighting business's operating income was minus 21 million euros in 2004, minus 6 in 2005, and plus 19 projected for 2006. Net income still negative, minus 3. A turnaround, but unfinished. The deal would be over 200 million dollars, about five times the electricals company's equity. And the electricals company's own growth is strong. Sales rose from 2,931 million rupees in fiscal 2003 to 11,151 million projected for 2006, about 3.8 times in three years. Walking away means staying in a domestic business squeezed by multinationals and Chinese imports, with no brand abroad. Buying means taking on a firm that is bigger, loss-making, run by corporate managers from a private equity background, while the electricals company is run on instinct and relationships.

Run Markides. Gate two fails partly, lighting technology and European brand management are missing. Gate four asks whether the lighting business's brands and managers will work once they are cut from private equity owners and moved under an Indian parent. Run the entry-mode grid. The lighting business sits at high value and high risk, which would suggest an alliance, but the target was for sale, so the electricals company faced buy or lose. The room tends to land on bid with discipline. Set a walk-away price before you start. Line up Deutsche Bank financing. Keep the lighting business's managers. Integrate lightly at first. And the case ends at the board meeting, so the outcome is not in the case. What stays with me is the joint managing director's question, can we handle people. It is a seven S question. The deal is not the risk. The fit afterwards is.

The third story is the eye hospital, March 2003. In 1976, its founder, a doctor, retires at 58 and opens an 11-bed hospital, five beds paying and six free. His mission, in the words of the case, is to eradicate needless blindness by providing appropriate, compassionate and high-quality eye care for all. By 2003 there are five hospitals with 3,649 beds, 2,850 of them free. Total surgeries rose from 123,095 in 1997 to 202,066 in 2003.

Here is the puzzle. How does a hospital treat most patients free and never borrow? The answer is an operating system. Paying patients are charged normal city rates, with the same doctors and the same quality. Each doctor performs about 2,500 to 2,600 surgeries a year, against 300 to 400 elsewhere. Two operating tables, four nurses per doctor, surgeries of around twelve minutes. Paramedics do tests, counsellors explain costs. Rural girls aged 17 to 19 are trained for two years, and 99 percent stay.

Then the corporate strategy move. The intraocular lens was costing 80 to 100 dollars imported. The binding constraint on cheap surgery was the price of the lens. So the eye hospital built its own lens factory, and now makes lenses for under 5 dollars. Around 600,000 lenses a year, a third exported. That is vertical integration, chosen because the input was the constraint. And a consulting arm, which had worked with 149 hospitals in India, Africa and Southeast Asia. The tension in 2003 is a question of scale. Build more hospitals of its own, or raise the productivity of other hospitals? The founder leans to strengthening existing hospitals. Leaders also worry about being too centralised in its home city. The rare asset, as one leader says, is values. Values are the real reason for efficiency. That is a seven S answer, and an inimitable asset in Markides' sense.

The fourth story is the rug company, March 2015. Its founder began with 5,000 rupees borrowed, a bicycle, two looms and nine weavers in Churu, Rajasthan. By 2015 the company has about 25,000 weavers in 11 states and 600 villages, exports to over 35 countries, and is the largest exporter of hand-knotted carpets from India. It removed contractors who used to pay weavers months late and unfairly.

But now there are two products, and they are really two businesses. Hand-knotted rugs sell for about 250 dollars per square metre in the United States, with gross margins of 30 to 35 percent, take two to eight months to weave, and last for decades. Tufted rugs sell for about 20 dollars per square metre, with margins of 5 to 10 percent, take five days, and last about five years. Hand-knotted is 37 percent of turnover, tufted 33 percent. Production cost is 40 percent labour and 35 percent raw material, leaving a margin of only about 5 to 6 percent against a target of 10.

Then the human tension. Professionals with typical for-profit values keep leaving, or are asked to leave. The founder identifies as a weaver. The family drafts non-negotiables, values kept even at the cost of profits. And one line from the case sits heavy. In the last few years, our profit has grown, but we have come away from our core. Should they focus on knotted, tufted, or both? One defensible answer is both, run as separate businesses with their own weaver pools, metrics and teams, with values written into pay and hiring, not only into meetings. Because the Markides lens says similar products can be different businesses, and the seven S lens says values must live in systems.

Now the traps. Let me name five, because these are the places where good students lose marks.

The first trap is treating diversification as a growth strategy. It is a parenting question. Growth is an outcome. The question is whether your company, as parent, makes the new business better. If the honest answer is that the new business would do just as well under any other owner, then you are adding cost, not value. So when someone says the market is growing, ask, growing for whom?

The second trap is counting synergies nobody has to deliver. A spreadsheet line saying cross-selling will add revenue is not a synergy. Someone must own it, in a sales team, with a target, and a pay system that rewards it. If you cannot name the person, you have a hope. And remember the formula. The premium must be smaller than the synergy, not smaller than the enthusiasm.

The third trap is confusing size with advantage. The electricals company buying a company twice its size can make it bigger overnight. Bigger is not better. The chipmaker's assembly and test plants show it plainly. Scale helps in fabs, but in assembly and test, gains come from learning. In the eye hospital, the advantage is not the number of beds. It is 2,500 surgeries per doctor, and a culture.

The fourth trap is thinking that relatedness is one thing. The electricals company and the lighting business look related. Knotted and tufted rugs look related. Even the word rug is the same. But relatedness has to be tested at the level of assets, the specific skills and channels that transfer. In the electricals case, market relatedness was high and technology relatedness was low. In the rugs case, the weavers, skills, design and marketing differ.

The fifth trap is choosing on data when the decision is strategic. In the chipmaker case, data screens, and strategy chooses. If you let the cheapest site on a spreadsheet win, you may end up with concentration risk or a host government that does not keep its promises. And the opposite trap is ignoring the hidden costs, which Porter and Rivkin list in detail.

And here is a sixth, a small one. Do not write the outcome of a case as if it is in the case. The chipmaker and electricals cases both stop before the answer. Use your notes, but say where they come from.
