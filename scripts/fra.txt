The finance head of a mid-sized auto parts company once stood up at a board meeting and said something that made everyone in the room stop chewing their samosas. He said, we made a healthy profit this year, and we are almost out of cash. Nobody laughed. The chairman had seen it happen before, and he knew exactly what was coming. Sales had grown nicely. The income statement, if you read only the top and the bottom line, looked like a company in fine health. But behind that healthy looking profit sat a warehouse full of unsold inventory, a pile of customer invoices nobody had collected on, and a bank account that had quietly thinned out over eleven months while the accountants kept smiling. This is the story that financial reporting and analysis exists to prevent. Not because anyone lied. Nobody had to lie. The rules of accrual accounting allowed every number on that income statement to be entirely correct and entirely misleading at the same time, because profit and cash are two different measurements of two different things, and a company can be doing beautifully on one and dying on the other without a single fraudulent entry anywhere in the ledger.

One more thing before we begin. This subject has a reputation for being dry, all debits and credits and column totals. It is not dry. It is closer to reading a patient's chart. The numbers a company reports are a story the company is choosing to tell about itself, and your job, as a manager, an investor, or simply someone deciding whether to trust a set of accounts, is to read that story the way a good doctor reads test results: not with suspicion for its own sake, but with the calm scepticism of someone who has seen healthy looking charts hide sick patients before. Let us begin.

Here is the one thread that runs underneath everything we will cover today. Accounting is not a record of cash. It is a record of promises, valued and timed according to a set of rules, and cash is the only thing that eventually settles those promises for real. Every topic in this course, the balance sheet, the income statement, the cash flow statement, ratio analysis, all of it exists because these two things, the promise and the settlement, are allowed to drift apart, sometimes for good economic reasons and sometimes because someone wanted the drift to happen.

Think about what a company actually does across a year. It ships goods before it is paid for them. It buys machinery that will be useful for a decade but writes a single cheque for it today. It owes its employees a bonus that will only be paid in April but earned in December. None of these are cash events at the moment they are recorded. Accrual accounting was invented to solve a real problem: if you only counted cash in and cash out, a single large customer payment landing on the thirty first of March would make a quiet month look spectacular, and a single large supplier payment would make a great month look disastrous. Accrual accounting tries to match effort with result, recording revenue when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, regardless of when the money physically moves. This is a genuine improvement over pure cash counting. It is also, and this is the part that the whole subject circles back to again and again, an invitation. Because the moment you allow judgement into the timing of revenue and expense, you allow the possibility that judgement gets used to flatter rather than to inform. Not usually through outright fraud. Usually through the hundred small choices a finance team makes every quarter: how fast to depreciate an asset, when exactly a sale counts as a sale, how to value inventory that has been sitting for a year. Every one of those choices is legal. Every one of those choices moves the reported number. And your entire job as a reader of financial statements is to notice where the choices were made and ask whether they make the company look better than it actually is, or whether they are simply the honest cost of doing business in a world where cash and promises do not arrive on the same day.

So hold that thought as we walk through the frameworks. Every tool coming up, the accounting equation, DuPont, free cash flow, is really just a different lens for looking at the same underlying tension: the gap between what a company says it earned and what it can actually put in the bank.

Let us start with the equation that never breaks, because everything else is built on top of it. Assets equal liabilities plus equity. Say it slowly. Everything a company owns, its cash, its buildings, its inventory, its machines, was paid for using one of exactly two sources of money: money the company borrowed, which is liabilities, or money the owners put in and left in the business, which is equity. There is no third bucket. There is no mysterious source of assets that belongs to neither the lenders nor the owners. This sounds almost too simple to matter, but it is the reason accounting fraud has to be creative rather than simply invisible. If you inflate an asset on one side of the ledger, something has to move on the other side to keep the equation balanced, and that something usually leaves a trace, a fake receivable that never gets collected, a liability that quietly disappears off the books into a shell entity, an equity figure that keeps climbing in ways the cash flow cannot support. Think of it like a household. Everything in your house, the furniture, the car, the flat itself, was paid for either with a loan you still owe or with your own savings that you put in. If someone asks how you suddenly own a new car with no loan and no dip in your savings, something in that story does not balance, and you would ask the same question of a company.

A quick business flavoured example. Imagine a small trading firm with one hundred crore rupees of assets on its balance sheet, made up of cash, inventory, and receivables. If sixty crore of that was financed by bank loans and supplier credit, the remaining forty crore must be equity, the owners money and accumulated profits. You do not need a separate calculation to know this. It falls straight out of the equation. Assets equal liabilities plus equity, one hundred equals sixty plus forty. When you reach for this framework, you reach for it constantly, every time you are handed a balance sheet and want a sanity check on whether the numbers hang together, and every time you want to understand how a company's growth was actually funded, through debt, or through ploughed back profit, or through fresh equity from investors.

So remember: assets equal liabilities plus equity, always, without exception, and the moment a set of numbers seems to violate that, either you are misreading the statement or someone wants you to.

Now the deeper framework, and the one the whole subject turns on: accrual versus cash. Let us build this from the ground up with a concrete story, because the words alone do not do it justice. Imagine you run a small catering business, and in March you cater a large wedding for a client, billing them two lakh rupees. You send the invoice on the thirtieth of March. Under accrual accounting, you record two lakh rupees of revenue in March, because that is when you delivered the service and earned the right to be paid. Your income statement for March looks wonderful. But your bank account tells a completely different story, because the client, following the fine old Indian tradition of paying vendors slowly, does not actually transfer the money until the end of May. For two full months, your books say you are profitable and your bank account says you are broke. Both statements are true at the same time. This is not a contradiction. It is the entire point of having two separate financial statements, the income statement, which tracks accrual, and the cash flow statement, which tracks money that has actually moved.

Now scale this up to the size of an actual company, and you can see how dangerous the gap becomes. A business can book rising revenue every single quarter, showing growing profit on paper, while its customers take longer and longer to pay, while its inventory piles up in a warehouse because sales are more promised than delivered, and while its own suppliers demand payment faster than its customers provide it. The income statement, quarter after quarter, tells a story of triumph. The bank account tells a story of a slow bleed. And the business can run out of cash and be forced to shut down, or be forced into a distress sale, while its own audited profit and loss statement insists that it was a successful, growing company the entire time. This is why any serious reader of financial statements holds two documents at once. Look at reported profit, then immediately look at operating cash flow for the same period, and ask why they differ. If profit is climbing steadily and cash flow is flat or falling, that gap has a story behind it, and the story is usually receivables growing faster than sales, or inventory growing faster than sales, or both. Neither of those is automatically fraud. Sometimes it is simply a company extending generous credit terms to win market share, which is a real business decision with real risk attached. But you, the reader, need to see it, name it, and decide whether you are comfortable with it, rather than being lulled by a rising profit line into believing everything is fine.

Pause here and try this yourself. A small business sells fifteen lakh rupees of goods in the month of November, all on credit, meaning no cash changes hands immediately. During the same month, the business collects eleven lakh rupees in cash from sales made back in October. It also pays six lakh rupees in cash for salaries and rent for November. Under accrual accounting, what is November's reported revenue, and what is November's actual net cash movement from these transactions? Think about it for a moment before I give you the answer. Under accrual accounting, November's revenue is fifteen lakh rupees, because that is what was earned and invoiced in November, regardless of when it gets collected. But the cash movement for November is eleven lakh rupees collected, minus six lakh rupees paid out, which is a positive five lakh rupees. So the accrual statement shows fifteen lakh of revenue, and the cash reality for the month is a much smaller five lakh rupees of net inflow, and that gap of ten lakh rupees is sitting on the balance sheet as a growing pile of receivables, money owed to the business that has not yet arrived. This is the exact mechanism, in miniature, of how a profitable looking company can quietly run low on cash.

When do you reach for this lens? Every single time. Whenever you are handed an income statement showing growth, your very next question should be, what does the cash flow statement say about the same period. It is the single highest value habit this course can give you.

So remember: accounting records promises, cash records money, and the two are allowed to disagree for a while, sometimes for good reasons, sometimes for concerning ones, and your job is always to look at both.

Now let us turn to the tool you will actually use for the rest of your working life, whether you become an investor, a banker, or a manager running your own business unit: the DuPont decomposition. Return on equity, the headline number that tells you how much profit a company generates for every rupee the owners have put in, can be broken into three separate engines multiplied together. The first engine is net profit margin, how much profit the company keeps out of every rupee of sales. The second engine is asset turnover, how many rupees of sales the company generates for every rupee of assets it employs. The third engine is financial leverage, how many rupees of assets the company controls for every rupee of equity the owners actually put in, in other words, how much of the balance sheet is built on borrowed money. Multiply margin, times turnover, times leverage, and you get return on equity. The beauty of this framework is that two companies can report the exact same headline return on equity while running on completely different machinery underneath, and if you only look at the headline number, you will never know the difference, and the difference matters enormously for risk.

Let me give you the everyday version first. Imagine two students both score ninety percent on the same exam. One of them studied efficiently over the semester, understood every concept, and walked in calm and prepared. The other pulled an all-nighter on borrowed notes, crammed everything in through sheer force, and got lucky that the questions matched what they memorised. Both walk away with the same ninety percent. But ask yourself which one you would trust to score ninety percent again next semester, under slightly different conditions, and the answer is obvious. Same score, completely different machine, completely different risk sitting underneath that identical number.

Now the business version, and this was the actual classroom comparison: the older shoemaker versus the younger rival, two footwear companies. When you decompose their return on equity using DuPont, the two companies land in strikingly different places on headline return on equity, with the older shoemaker coming in at roughly twenty one and a half percent and the younger rival running noticeably higher, at roughly thirty two and a half percent. Now here is the interesting part, the part that DuPont exists to reveal. The two companies had reasonably similar profit margins, and reasonably similar asset turnover, meaning they were converting sales into profit at broadly comparable rates, and generating sales from their asset base at broadly comparable rates. The gap in their return on equity was not coming primarily from being a better run business in the operational sense. It was coming almost entirely from the third engine, leverage. The younger rival was operating with a meaningfully more leveraged balance sheet than the older shoemaker, more borrowed capital relative to owners equity, and that leverage was doing the heavy lifting in pushing return on equity higher. This is not automatically a criticism of the younger rival, leverage is a legitimate tool and it can be used sensibly. But it means that an investor comparing the two companies purely on headline return on equity, without decomposing it, would conclude that the younger rival is simply the better business, when the honest conclusion is that the younger rival is running a similar business on a different, riskier capital structure. If sales ever slow down sharply, the more leveraged company feels the pain first and feels it harder, because interest and debt obligations do not shrink just because revenue does.

Pause here and try this yourself, with a clean invented example so the arithmetic is simple. A company has a net profit margin of eight percent, an asset turnover of one point five times, meaning it generates one and a half rupees of sales for every rupee of assets, and a leverage ratio, assets divided by equity, of two times. What is its return on equity? Multiply the three engines together. Eight percent, times one point five, times two. Eight percent times one point five gives you twelve percent. Twelve percent times two gives you twenty four percent. So this company's return on equity is twenty four percent, and now you can see exactly how much of that twenty four percent is coming from genuine operating performance, the margin and turnover pieces, versus how much is coming from simply borrowing more, the leverage piece. If a rival company reported the same twenty four percent return on equity but with a leverage ratio of four instead of two, you would immediately know that rival is a riskier business wearing the same headline number as a costume.

When do you reach for DuPont? Whenever a headline return on equity or return on assets is being used to compare two companies, or to argue that a company's performance has improved or worsened. Never accept the headline number alone. Break it into its three engines and ask which one moved, because margin improving means the business got more efficient, turnover improving means the assets are working harder, and leverage increasing means the owners are simply taking on more risk to produce the same looking result.

So remember: return on equity is margin, times turnover, times leverage, and two companies can wear the same headline number while running completely different, and differently risky, machines underneath.

Next, free cash flow, which is arguably the most honest number on the entire set of financial statements, because it is the hardest one to dress up. Start with operating cash flow, the actual cash a company's core business generated during the period, which already strips out a good deal of the accrual noise we discussed earlier. Now subtract capital expenditure, the money the company must spend on new or replacement equipment, buildings, and infrastructure simply to keep the existing business running, not to grow it, just to keep it standing still. What remains after that subtraction is free cash flow, and it represents what the business has genuinely generated for its owners, money that is truly free to be paid out as dividends, used to pay down debt, or reinvested in growth, rather than money that is trapped in keeping the lights on.

The everyday version of this is your own monthly salary. Your salary is not the number that matters for your personal financial health. What matters is your salary minus the unavoidable costs of simply functioning, the scooter repair you cannot skip, the medical expense that cannot wait, the rent that is due regardless of how your month went. What is left after those unavoidable costs is what you can actually save or spend freely, and that is your personal free cash flow.

In business terms, imagine a manufacturing company reporting rising net profit for three straight years, a trend that looks fantastic in the annual report. But suppose during those same three years, the company's operating cash flow stayed roughly flat, while its capital expenditure kept climbing because its factory equipment was ageing and needed constant replacement just to maintain current output. In that scenario, free cash flow could actually be shrinking, or even turning negative, even as reported profit rises every year. A company in that position is, in a very real sense, consuming capital rather than creating it. It looks successful on the income statement while it is quietly eating its own foundation, and shareholders who only read the profit line would have no idea.

Pause here and try this yourself. A company reports operating cash flow of ninety lakh rupees for the year. Its capital expenditure for the same year, the amount it had to spend maintaining its plant and equipment, was sixty lakh rupees. What is its free cash flow? Ninety lakh minus sixty lakh is thirty lakh rupees. That thirty lakh rupees is the genuinely free cash the business generated, the amount actually available for dividends, debt repayment, or true growth investment, as opposed to maintenance spending the company has no real choice about.

When do you reach for free cash flow? Whenever profit has been rising for several years in a row and you want to check whether that growth is real or whether it is being funded by an ageing asset base that will eventually demand its money back. Free cash flow rarely lies the way reported profit occasionally can, because it is much harder to manufacture cash out of accounting choices than it is to manufacture profit.

So remember: free cash flow is operating cash flow minus the capital spending the business cannot avoid, and a company can report growing profit for years while its free cash flow quietly goes negative, which is one of the clearest early warning signs in all of financial analysis.

Finally, a habit rather than a formula, and it is this: read the accounting policies before you read the numbers. Every set of financial statements comes with notes, usually near the back, that disclose the choices management made, how fast assets are depreciated, when exactly revenue is recognised as earned, which method is used to value inventory when prices are changing. These are not neutral technical footnotes. Each one is a lever that moves the reported numbers, sometimes by a small amount, sometimes enormously, and each lever was pulled by a person or a team who had reasons, and reasons attach to incentives. A company under pressure to show strong quarterly results has a motive to recognise revenue a little earlier, to depreciate assets a little more slowly so profit looks better this year at the cost of looking worse later, or to value inventory in a way that flatters the current period. None of this needs to be illegal. It simply needs to be understood.

This is exactly the terrain the auto supplier case in class was built to explore. On the surface, the case is about a single underlying business. But depending on which accounting policy lens you apply to that business, the story you can tell about its performance changes substantially, sometimes dramatically, even though nothing about the actual operations has changed at all. The same trucks were sold, the same customers paid, the same factory ran the same shifts. What changed was the accounting choice describing all of it, and that choice alone was enough to shift the picture of how the business was performing. The lesson generalises far beyond this one case: whenever you are comparing two companies, or comparing one company across different years, check whether the accounting policies are actually the same before you compare the numbers, because comparing a company that depreciates assets over five years against one that depreciates over ten years is not really comparing business performance at all, it is comparing bookkeeping choices.

So remember: numbers do not arrive from nowhere, they arrive from choices, and every choice has an author, and every author has incentives, so read the policy notes before you trust the headline figures.

Now let us walk through the two case stories in full, because this is where these ideas stop being abstractions and become something you can actually picture.

Start with the older shoemaker and the younger rival. Both are well known footwear companies in India, both sell shoes to broadly similar customers, and if you only glanced at their headline return on equity, you would conclude that the younger rival, at roughly thirty two and a half percent, was simply running rings around the older shoemaker, sitting at roughly twenty one and a half percent. That is an eleven percentage point gap, which in the world of listed companies is enormous, the kind of gap that would make an investor rush to buy one stock and avoid the other. But the class exercise was to actually decompose both numbers using DuPont, breaking each company's return on equity into its three engines: net margin, asset turnover, and leverage. When the two companies were placed side by side engine by engine, the story changed completely. Their profit margins were in a broadly similar range, meaning neither company was dramatically better at converting a rupee of sales into a rupee of profit. Their asset turnover figures were also broadly comparable, meaning neither company was dramatically more efficient at squeezing sales out of its asset base. The engine that actually explained almost the entire gap between twenty one and a half percent and thirty two and a half percent was leverage. The younger rival was simply operating with meaningfully more debt relative to its equity base than the older shoemaker was. More borrowed capital funding a similar operating business meant a smaller equity base dividing into a similar or proportionate profit pool, and that arithmetic alone, leverage amplifying returns to equity, accounted for most of the difference. The lesson the professor wanted students to walk away with was not that leverage is bad, and not that the younger rival made a mistake. Leverage is a completely legitimate tool, used carefully it can be entirely sensible. The lesson was that a headline return on equity number, taken alone, actively hides this information from you. Two businesses that are operationally quite similar can present wildly different headline returns purely because one of them is taking on more financial risk, and unless you decompose the number, you will mistake financial risk for operational excellence, which is exactly the mistake an unsophisticated investor makes every single day in every stock market in the world.

Now the second story, the auto supplier, which lives entirely inside the accrual versus cash distinction we spent so much time on earlier. The case presents students with a business, an auto sector supplier, and asks them to evaluate its performance. On the surface, using standard accrual accounting figures, the business tells one story about how it has been doing. But the case pushes students to also examine the business through the lens of cash movement, actual money in and actual money out, rather than accrued promises. And when you switch lenses, from the accrual story to the cash story, the picture of the business's health shifts, sometimes quite significantly, even though the underlying operations, the actual trucks manufactured, the actual parts shipped, the actual customers served, never changed at all. What changed was simply which side of the accrual and cash divide you chose to stand on while judging the business. The pedagogical point is blunt and important: the same company, the same year, the same operations, can be described as thriving or as struggling depending on whether you are reading the profit and loss statement in isolation or reading it alongside the cash flow statement, and a manager, an investor, or a lender who only ever looks at one of those two documents is only ever getting half the truth, and often the more flattering half, because accrual accounting, applied by people who want the numbers to look good, tends to drift toward optimism long before it drifts toward caution.

Put these two case stories side by side and you can see they are really teaching the same underlying discipline from two different angles. The older shoemaker and the younger rival teach you never to trust a single ratio without decomposing it. The auto supplier teaches you never to trust a single financial statement without checking it against the other. Both cases are, underneath the specific numbers, the same warning: the first number you see is never the whole story, and the discipline of financial analysis is the discipline of insisting on the second look.

Now, the traps, the places where even careful, intelligent people go wrong.

The first trap is assuming that rising profit means rising cash. We have spent a great deal of this episode dismantling that assumption, and it deserves to be named directly as a trap because it is the single most common misreading of financial statements among people who have not been trained to separate the two. Rising profit is good news that requires a second question, not good news that ends the conversation.

The second trap is assuming that a higher return on equity automatically means a better run business. As the older shoemaker and the younger rival showed us, a higher return on equity can simply mean a more leveraged balance sheet, which is a different thing entirely, carrying different risk, and deserving a different kind of respect.

The third trap is treating accounting policy notes as boring boilerplate to be skipped. They are, in fact, the single richest source of information about how much to trust the headline numbers sitting above them, and skipping them is like skipping the ingredients list because you trust the smell of the food.

The fourth trap is believing that because a set of accounts has been audited, it must be free of judgement calls. Audits check that the rules were followed and that the arithmetic is consistent. They do not certify that every judgement call, how fast to depreciate, when exactly to recognise revenue, was made in the most conservative or most honest way possible. Two entirely legitimate, entirely audited sets of accounts can tell meaningfully different stories about the same business.

And the fifth trap, perhaps the subtlest, is assuming that because no fraud occurred, no distortion occurred. Most misleading financial statements are not the product of fraud. They are the product of dozens of small, individually defensible choices, each one nudging the picture slightly, that compound into a headline number that no longer tells you what you think it tells you. You do not need a villain for this to happen. You just need incentives, judgement, and enough small choices piling up in the same direction.

Let us do the rapid recap. Assets equal liabilities plus equity, always, without exception, and that equation is the skeleton every other number hangs on. Accrual accounting records promises, cash records money, and the two are allowed to disagree, sometimes for good reasons and sometimes for concerning ones, so always read the income statement and the cash flow statement together. DuPont breaks return on equity into margin, turnover, and leverage, and the older shoemaker versus the younger rival showed us that a big gap in headline return on equity can come almost entirely from leverage rather than from genuinely better operations. Free cash flow, operating cash flow minus unavoidable capital spending, is one of the hardest numbers to dress up, and it can quietly turn negative even while reported profit keeps climbing. And accounting policy choices, depreciation, revenue recognition, inventory valuation, are levers with authors and incentives behind them, exactly the terrain the auto supplier case walked us through, where the same business told two different stories depending on which lens you used to look at it.

Your one sentence for financial reporting and analysis: profit is an opinion, cash is a fact, and DuPont tells you which engine, margin, turnover, or leverage, is actually producing the headline number in front of you.
