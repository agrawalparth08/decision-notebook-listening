It is ten thirty at night on the twenty first of March, two thousand fifteen, and Ariana Grande has just finished a sold out show at Madison Square Garden in New York. Nineteen thousand people are pouring out of the arena at once, and almost all of them want to go home right now. Inside Uber's systems, something interesting starts to happen. In the space of a few minutes, the number of people opening the Uber app in that neighbourhood jumps to four times the normal level. Every one of them is doing the same thing, checking if a car is close by.

Now think about what happens next if nothing changes. Demand has quadrupled, but the number of drivers on the road near the Garden that night is roughly what it always is on a Saturday. That is not a small mismatch, that is a wall. Someone has to wait a very long time, or not get a ride at all.

What Uber's system did instead was raise the price. Over the next seventy five minutes, the fare near the Garden multiplied by anywhere from one point two to one point eight times the normal rate, moving up and down as the crowd thinned out. And here is the part worth sitting with. Within minutes, the number of active drivers in that same patch of Manhattan roughly doubled. Drivers who were at home, or finishing a trip across town, saw the surge on their own screens and pointed their cars toward Midtown. The price did not just ration who got a ride. It also pulled in the very supply that was missing.

By the end of the night, the average wait for a car in that surge zone was two and a half minutes. Not two and a half hours, two and a half minutes, in a fifteen block radius with nineteen thousand freshly displaced concertgoers. That is the kind of result that a fixed price, however fair it sounds, could not have delivered that night.

Let me start with the one thread, because once you see it, the rest of this episode is really just six different camera angles on the same scene.

Here it is. A price is not a moral judgment. It is a piece of information about scarcity, travelling at the speed of a number.

I want you to sit with that for a second, because most of our instincts run the other way. When the price of something jumps, our gut says someone is being greedy, someone is taking advantage. Sometimes that is even true. But the more useful question, the economist's question, is what is this price telling the market to do right now. A high price is the market shouting that a thing is temporarily scarce, and that shout does two jobs simultaneously. It tells the people who want the thing least urgently to wait, or to walk away. And it tells anyone who could supply more of the thing, right now, tonight, that this is a very good moment to show up. Nobody has to coordinate this. Nobody sends a memo. The price does both jobs on its own, the moment it moves.

This is why the whole subject keeps returning to the same handful of questions in different costumes. How much does a price change actually change behaviour. That is elasticity. How far can a price rise above cost before it chases customers away entirely. That is market power and the Lerner index. Should a firm cut its price to keep the lights on, or is it better to switch the lights off. That is the shutdown rule. Should a manager keep pouring money into a project because of what has already been spent. That is the sunk cost question, and the honest answer is that the past has no vote, only the future has a vote. Should a firm let a surge in price do the rationing during a crunch, instead of a queue or a lottery. That is the allocation question the Uber story answers so cleanly. And finally, when you cannot watch your people every hour of the day, what do you pay them for, and what do you accidentally teach them to ignore. That is the principal agent problem.

Six frameworks, one thread. Every one of them is a different way of asking, what is the price, or the incentive, actually telling people to do, and is that the outcome we actually want.

One more thing before we move into the tools themselves. Microeconomics has a reputation for being cold, all numbers and no humanity. I want to push back on that a little. Every framework in this course is really a study of how ordinary people respond to the situation they are placed in. The teacher who stops teaching what is not tested. The driver who drives toward the surge. The manager who cannot let go of a project because letting go feels like admitting failure. These are deeply human responses to structure. Microeconomics just gives you the vocabulary to see the structure clearly, so you can either fix it, or work with it instead of against it.

Now let us build the toolkit.

The first tool is price elasticity of demand, and it answers the single most practical question in business, if I change my price, will my revenue go up or down.

Here is the plain version. When you cut a price, two things happen at once, pulling in opposite directions. More people buy, because it is cheaper. But every person who was already going to buy now pays you less. Whether your total revenue rises or falls depends entirely on which of those two forces is bigger. Elasticity is just the name we give to that comparison. If a small price cut brings in a much larger jump in volume, demand is elastic, and the cut pays for itself many times over. If the price cut brings in only a small extra trickle of volume, demand is inelastic, and you have simply handed a discount to people who would have paid full price anyway.

Think about salt. If the price of salt in your kitchen doubled tomorrow, would you buy noticeably less of it. Almost certainly not. You use a pinch, it barely dents your budget, and there is no real substitute for the flavour it gives. Salt is inelastic. Now think about which airline you fly from Mumbai to Bangalore on a Tuesday morning, when four airlines run the same route at the same time. If one airline's fare rises even slightly above the others, a large share of travellers will simply click on a different name. That demand is elastic, because substitutes are sitting right there, one tab away.

Now put real numbers on it, the way you would in a business review. Say a company sells a product at one hundred rupees, and at that price it sells ten thousand units a month, giving revenue of ten lakh rupees. The finance team proposes a ten percent price cut, to ninety rupees. If the cut is a hit and volume rises by twenty five percent, to twelve thousand five hundred units, the new revenue is ninety times twelve thousand five hundred, which is eleven lakh twenty five thousand rupees. The cut worked, because the volume response outran the price cut. But suppose instead that volume only rises by five percent, to ten thousand five hundred units. New revenue is ninety times ten thousand five hundred, which is nine lakh forty five thousand rupees. Same price cut, same intention, and the company is now worse off by fifty five thousand rupees a month, because demand did not move enough to compensate. Same action, opposite outcome, and the only difference between the two stories is the elasticity of that particular customer base for that particular product.

Pause here and try this yourself. A bookshop sells a bestselling novel at five hundred rupees and moves two hundred copies a week. The owner considers cutting the price by twenty percent, to four hundred rupees. For the cut to be worth doing on revenue alone, roughly how much would weekly volume need to rise. Work it out before you keep listening.

Here is the talked through answer. At five hundred rupees and two hundred copies, weekly revenue is one lakh rupees. At four hundred rupees, to simply match that one lakh rupees in revenue, the shop would need to sell two hundred fifty copies, because four hundred times two hundred fifty is exactly one lakh. That is a twenty five percent rise in volume needed just to break even on revenue, for a twenty percent cut in price. Anything less than a twenty five percent volume increase, and the price cut has actually cost the shop money, even though the shelves look busier. This is exactly why the busiest looking sale in a shop can quietly be the least profitable day of the month.

Reach for elasticity whenever anyone proposes a price change, a discount, a markdown, or a premium tier, and cannot tell you what they expect volume to do in response. If nobody in the room has an elasticity number, or at least an informed guess, the pricing decision is really a guess dressed up as a decision.

So remember, a price change is only good news if the volume response, in percentage terms, is bigger than the price change itself, and elasticity is simply the name for that comparison.

The second tool tells you how much markup a firm can actually sustain, and the answer is less about cleverness than about structure.

Here is the plain version. Your ability to charge more than your cost is capped by how easily your customers can walk away. If customers have almost nowhere else to go, you can charge a large markup and they will still buy. If customers have five other options within arm's reach, your markup gets squeezed down close to your cost, no matter how good your product is. Economists call this the Lerner index, and the intuition is simple even if the name sounds technical, your pricing power is the mirror image of your customer's elasticity.

Think about the tea stall right outside an exam hall on the morning of a difficult exam. There might be one stall, a hundred anxious students, and nowhere else to go without missing the exam. That stall can charge nearly double its usual price for a cup of chai, and it will sell out. Now walk two hundred metres to the railway station, where ten tea stalls sit in a row, each one visible from the next. None of them can meaningfully overcharge, because a customer who feels gouged simply walks three stalls down. Same product, same ingredients, wildly different pricing power, purely because of how many alternatives sit within reach.

Now the business version, because this shows up constantly in strategy discussions. Two companies sell what looks like the same category of product. Company A operates in a market with three serious competitors, tight substitutes, and low switching costs. Company B has built a genuinely different product, high switching costs, and few real substitutes. Even if both companies have identical costs of ten rupees per unit, Company A might only be able to sustain a price of twelve rupees, a markup of twenty percent, before customers defect. Company B, facing much weaker competitive pressure, might sustain a price of eighteen rupees on the same ten rupee cost, a markup of eighty percent. The difference is not that Company B's managers are smarter or work harder. The difference is structural. Company B faces a customer base with far less elastic demand, and the Lerner index simply makes that visible as a number.

This is the idea that returns, almost word for word, in the strategy classroom, where you will hear it dressed up as competitive moats, differentiation, and switching costs. Underneath all of that language is the same arithmetic you just heard, markup is capped by elasticity, and elasticity is shaped by how many real alternatives your customer has.

Reach for this framework whenever you are asked to defend a pricing decision, or whenever a competitor's high margin puzzles you. Before assuming they are simply better operators, ask what structural feature, patents, network effects, high switching costs, geography, is keeping their customers from walking away.

So remember, no firm sets its own margin by willpower, the market sets a ceiling, and that ceiling is fixed by how easily the customer can leave.

The third tool is the shutdown rule, and it saves companies from a very specific kind of slow, quiet failure.

Here is the plain version. In the short run, a firm has already paid its fixed costs, its rent, its loan instalments, its long term leases. Those costs exist whether the firm produces one unit or a million. So the real question a struggling firm should ask is not am I profitable overall, but does each additional unit I sell bring in more cash than it costs to make. If the price you receive is still above your variable cost per unit, keep operating, even while running an overall loss, because every unit you sell is contributing something toward those fixed costs that you are paying regardless. But the moment your price falls below your variable cost, every single unit you sell is now making the hole deeper, not shallower. At that point, and only at that point, shut down.

Think of a small restaurant paying forty thousand rupees a month in rent, whether it opens its doors or not. If the restaurant's price per meal covers the cost of the ingredients and the cook's hourly wage, with something left over, it should stay open even on a slow month where total profit is negative, because the alternative, staying shut, means paying the full forty thousand rupees in rent with zero revenue coming in at all. Something toward the rent beats nothing toward the rent.

Now the sharper business version. Imagine a small manufacturing unit with fixed costs of five lakh rupees a month, and a variable cost of two hundred rupees per unit. If the market price falls to two hundred and fifty rupees per unit due to a downturn, the unit should keep running. Every unit sold contributes fifty rupees toward the fixed costs, so producing and selling ten thousand units in that month brings in five lakh rupees toward fixed costs of five lakh rupees, nearly breaking even on cash, which is far better than shutting the plant and paying the five lakh rupees in fixed costs with zero contribution at all. But if the market price collapses further, to one hundred and eighty rupees per unit, below the two hundred rupee variable cost, the arithmetic flips completely. Now every single unit sold loses twenty rupees on top of the fixed costs already owed. At that price, running the plant does not reduce the loss, it actively grows it, unit by unit. That is exactly when a manager should shut the line down, at least temporarily, and take the fixed cost loss without adding a variable one on top.

Reach for this rule whenever a business unit, a product line, or even a small side venture is losing money and someone is debating whether to close it. The question is never simply is it profitable. The question is does price cover variable cost. Those are very different questions, and confusing them causes both premature shutdowns and slow bleeds that should have stopped months earlier.

So remember, keep running as long as price covers variable cost, because something toward the rent beats nothing, and stop the instant price falls below it, because then every sale is a self inflicted wound.

The fourth tool is the discipline of ignoring, and I want to spend real time on it, because it is the single most common mistake I have watched intelligent people make, repeatedly, in rooms where a great deal of money was at stake.

Here is the plain version. A sunk cost is money, time, or effort that has already been spent and cannot be recovered, no matter what you decide to do next. The rule is simple to state and brutally hard to follow, a sunk cost should have no influence whatsoever on your decision about what to do going forward. Only future costs and future benefits matter. The past has already happened. It does not get a vote.

You know this feeling already, even if you have never named it. You buy a movie ticket, you sit down, and twenty minutes in, the film is clearly terrible. Do you stay because you already paid for the ticket. Most people do, and it is completely irrational. The money for the ticket is gone whether you stay or leave. Staying does not bring it back. The only real question left is whether the next hour and forty minutes of your evening, which you have not spent yet, is better used watching a bad film or doing literally anything else. The ticket price should not enter that calculation at all, and yet it dominates most people's thinking in that exact moment.

Now the business version, where the stakes are much higher than a movie ticket. Imagine a company has spent forty lakh rupees developing a new product over eighteen months. Market research now suggests the product will not be a hit, and finishing it will require another twelve lakh rupees. Once finished, the company expects it will earn fifteen lakh rupees in total sales before being discontinued. Many managers, faced with this, say something like, we cannot walk away now, we have already put in forty lakh rupees. But that forty lakh rupees is gone regardless of what happens next. It should not appear anywhere in this decision. The only numbers that belong in the decision are the twelve lakh rupees still to be spent, and the fifteen lakh rupees still to be earned. Twelve lakh in, fifteen lakh out, a positive return of three lakh rupees on the money not yet spent. On that basis alone, finishing the product is the right call, not because of the forty lakh already sunk, but despite it being completely irrelevant to the choice.

Here is the twist that makes this framework so slippery. Sometimes walking away really is correct, and sunk cost thinking wrongly keeps people locked into a bad project purely because abandoning it feels like admitting the money was wasted. It was wasted, the moment it was spent, regardless of what you decide next. The only question that should ever follow a sunk cost is, given where we stand right now, forgetting everything already spent, is the path ahead still worth walking.

Pause here and try this yourself. A company has spent eighty lakh rupees building a machine for a new production line. To finish installation will cost another twenty lakh rupees. Once running, the machine is expected to generate thirty lakh rupees in extra profit over its life. A rival technology has just appeared on the market that would do the same job better, but adopting it means abandoning the eighty lakh rupees already spent and starting again at a cost of forty lakh rupees, with an expected profit of ninety lakh rupees. Which path creates more value from this point forward, and does the eighty lakh already spent belong in your answer.

Here is the talked through answer. The eighty lakh rupees is sunk in both paths, so it cancels out and does not belong in the comparison at all. Finishing the existing machine costs twenty lakh rupees more and returns thirty lakh rupees, a net gain of ten lakh rupees from here forward. Switching to the new technology costs forty lakh rupees and returns ninety lakh rupees, a net gain of fifty lakh rupees from here forward. Comparing only the future numbers, switching is clearly the better decision, by a wide margin, even though it means writing off eighty lakh rupees that was already spent. Most managers in the room, focused on not wasting the eighty lakh, would have finished the old machine and left forty lakh rupees of value on the table.

Reach for this discipline whenever you hear the phrase we have already invested so much, or we cannot stop now. Those sentences are usually the sound of a sunk cost quietly hijacking a decision that should be made on future numbers alone.

So remember, money already spent has no vote in what happens next, the only question worth asking is whether the road ahead, on its own, is worth walking.

The fifth tool is understanding price as an allocation mechanism, and this is the one that makes sense of situations that feel, on the surface, deeply unfair.

Here is the plain version. When demand for something suddenly outstrips supply, there are only a few ways to decide who gets it. You can use a queue, first come first served. You can use a lottery, random chance. You can use rationing by authority, someone decides. Or you can let the price rise, and let the price itself sort people by how urgently they need the thing right now, while simultaneously pulling in more of the thing wherever that is possible. None of these methods is free of trade offs, but price has one advantage the others do not, it does two jobs at once, sorting demand and summoning supply, without anyone having to coordinate it by hand.

Think about a single umbrella stall on a street where it suddenly starts pouring rain. If the stall keeps its price fixed, the first ten people in line get an umbrella and everyone after that gets nothing, regardless of how badly they need to stay dry, a wedding guest and someone just popping out for milk are treated identically by the queue. If instead the price rises sharply the moment the rain starts, some people who only wanted to nip out briefly will decide to wait it out instead, freeing up umbrellas for people who genuinely cannot delay, and word may spread fast enough that another vendor rushes umbrellas over from two streets away to catch the high price, adding supply exactly when it is needed most.

This is precisely the logic behind Uber's surge pricing, which we return to shortly with its full numbers. But the point to hold onto here is this. A rising price during a shortage is not simply a company extracting more money from desperate customers, even though it can feel that way in the moment. It nudges the person who can easily wait, or take the bus, to step back from the queue, freeing up the scarce resource for someone who genuinely cannot substitute away from it right now. And it signals to anyone who could supply more, right now, that this is the moment to show up. A fixed price during a shortage does neither job. It just produces a longer line and an empty shelf.

None of this means every price surge is fair, or that people are wrong to feel angry about paying triple for a ride home. There are real questions about vulnerability, about whether some goods, insulin, floodwater, a hospital bed, should ever be allocated by price at all. But object on the right grounds, fairness, vulnerability, the specific good in question, rather than claiming the price is doing no useful economic work. It usually is.

Reach for this framework whenever a shortage appears, a festival, a natural disaster, a product launch, and someone proposes fixing the price to protect customers. Ask what will happen to the queue, and ask where the additional supply is supposed to come from if the price is not allowed to move.

So remember, a rising price in a shortage is quietly doing two jobs at once, rationing demand and summoning supply, and a fixed price in the same shortage does neither.

The sixth and final tool is my favourite in the entire course, because it explains something that trips up almost every manager at some point, why the people working for you so often deliver exactly what you measured, and quietly abandon everything you forgot to measure.

Here is the plain version. A principal owns something, a company, a classroom of students, a hospital, and delegates the work of running it to an agent, a manager, a teacher, a surgeon. The principal cannot watch the agent every hour of every day. So the principal pays the agent based on outcomes that can actually be observed and measured. That sounds sensible, and often it is. But here is the catch. The agent will optimise for exactly what is measured, and only for what is measured, because that is what determines their pay, their promotion, their standing. Anything valuable that falls outside the measurement quietly gets deprioritised, not out of malice, but because nobody is rewarding it.

Think about a simple household example. Pay a house painter by the room, and they will paint quickly, with quality slipping on the corners, because speed earns them more rooms per day. Pay them by the hour, and the corners get more attention, but now you must worry they work slower than necessary, because time itself earns them money. Neither contract is perfect, because a single number can never capture everything you actually care about at once.

Now the case that makes this vivid at scale. In rural Andhra Pradesh, researchers ran an experiment across five hundred government schools in five districts, splitting them into groups: individual teacher bonus, group bonus, an extra contract teacher, a free cash grant for the principal, and a final comparison group with no intervention at all. The bonus itself was five hundred rupees multiplied by the percentage gain in average test scores, once a minimum improvement threshold of five percent was crossed.

After two years, the results told a very specific story about what was measured. Students in incentive schools outperformed the comparison schools clearly, and individually incentivised schools outperformed group incentivised schools, likely because a shared bonus lets one teacher's extra effort get diluted across an entire staff room of colleagues. And in a genuinely encouraging twist, the gains were not confined to math and Telugu, the two subjects that were actually part of the bonus formula. Science and social studies scores rose too, even though no bonus was tied to them, suggesting that teachers who were rewarded for outcomes in two subjects ended up simply becoming better, more engaged teachers across the board.

But teacher attendance, the very problem that first drew researchers to Andhra Pradesh's classrooms, showed no significant change at all. Teachers who were already showing up regularly worked harder and taught better. Teachers who were in the habit of skipping school kept skipping school, because nothing in the bonus formula touched whether they walked through the gate in the morning. The incentive was precisely engineered around test scores, and it moved test scores. It said nothing about attendance, and attendance simply refused to move. What gets measured gets managed. What gets left out gets quietly abandoned, even by people responding rationally and honestly to the incentive placed in front of them.

Reach for this framework any time you are designing someone's compensation, a salesperson's target, a call centre agent's bonus. Ask two questions before you finalise the number. What will this measurement make people do more of, and what will it make people quietly stop doing, because it was never on the scoreboard in the first place.

So remember, you will get exactly what you measure, more of it than you expected, and you will lose whatever you forgot to measure, often without anyone even noticing until much later.

Now let us slow down and sit properly inside two full stories, because both of them turn abstract ideas into something you can actually picture.

Return with me to the twenty first of March, two thousand fifteen, ten thirty at night, outside Madison Square Garden. Ariana Grande has just finished her show, and nineteen thousand people are streaming out onto the avenues at almost exactly the same moment, most of them reaching for their phones. Inside Uber's data, the picture is stark. App openings in that neighbourhood spike to roughly four times the pre concert baseline within minutes. This is not a gentle rise, it is a wall of demand arriving all at once, the kind that no ordinary Saturday prepares a system for.

At a fixed price, the number of drivers in that fifteen block radius would not have changed instantly just because a concert let out, since drivers had no way of knowing the venue was emptying unless something told them. With demand at four times normal and supply roughly unchanged, most people trying to get a ride that night would have waited an extremely long time, or simply not gotten one at all. A fixed price cannot conjure a single extra driver out of thin air.

What actually happened is the surge algorithm kicked in, and over the following seventy five minutes, the fare in that zone moved through several steps, from one point two times the normal fare, through one point three, one point four, one point five, all the way to one point eight times normal at its peak, spending roughly thirty five of those seventy five minutes at some surged level before settling back down. Two things happened simultaneously, and both mattered.

First, on the demand side, not everyone who opened the app and saw a surged price chose to request a ride. Some decided to wait for the surge to ease. Some chose the subway instead. The number of actual ride requests rose by far less than the four times jump in app openings, which meant the system was quietly filtering for the riders who valued getting home right now the most, rather than serving whoever happened to open the app in the first sixty seconds.

Second, and this is the part that genuinely surprised people the first time they saw the data, the number of active drivers in that same patch of Manhattan roughly doubled during the surge window. Drivers elsewhere in the city, or drivers who had just dropped off a fare and were deciding where to head next, saw the elevated fare on their own screens and steered toward Midtown. The price was not just filtering demand, it was actively summoning supply that had not existed in that location a few minutes earlier.

The result showed up in two numbers that Uber itself considers the true test of whether the system worked. The completion rate, the share of requested rides that actually got fulfilled, barely moved at all despite the flood of demand. And the average wait time for a car during that surge period came out to two and a half minutes. Not two and a half hours, two and a half minutes, for a crowd of nineteen thousand freshly displaced concertgoers competing for cars in real time. One more number worth holding onto, drivers working in that surge zone during those seventy five minutes earned roughly thirteen percent more than they would have at the normal fare, which is precisely the incentive that pulled the extra supply toward Madison Square Garden in the first place rather than toward some other, unsurged part of the city.

None of this erases the very real frustration riders feel paying nearly double the normal fare on a tired night. But the alternative was a much longer queue, a lower completion rate, and no extra drivers showing up at all. The surge was not punishing anyone. It was doing, quietly and automatically, the allocation work that a queue or a lottery cannot do nearly as well.

Now travel from a Manhattan sidewalk to a very different setting, five hundred government schools spread across five rural districts of Andhra Pradesh, East Godavari, Kadapa, Medak, Nizamabad, and Visakhapatnam, in the years following two thousand five. At that point, barely a quarter of all fifth grade students could read a simple first grade level text, and fewer than a third could do basic subtraction. Teacher absenteeism across the state hovered around a quarter of all teachers on any given day, in a system where almost all teachers were permanent government employees, strongly unionised, on largely fixed salaries with essentially no risk of being fired for poor performance. The average teacher earned around eight thousand rupees a month, and that pay barely moved whether a teacher was excellent or absent half the term.

Two researchers, one an economics professor and one an economist at the World Bank, designed a careful experiment to test whether ideas borrowed from corporate incentive design could move the needle. They randomly split the five hundred schools into five equal groups. In the first, individual teachers earned a bonus tied directly to how much their own students' scores improved. In the second, all teachers at a school shared a group bonus tied to the whole school's improvement. The bonus formula in both cases was the same, five hundred rupees for every percentage point of average improvement in math and Telugu scores, once a minimum five percent improvement threshold had been crossed. The third and fourth groups received an extra contract teacher and a cash grant respectively, and the fifth group received nothing at all, serving as the honest baseline.

Two years later, students in incentive schools clearly outperformed the comparison schools, and individually incentivised schools outperformed group incentivised schools, likely because a shared bonus lets one teacher's effort get diluted across an entire staff room. The gains were not even confined to math and Telugu, the two subjects tied to the bonus. Science and social studies scores rose too, even though no bonus touched them, suggesting teachers simply became better, more engaged teachers across the board.

But teacher attendance, the very problem that first drew researchers to these classrooms, showed no significant change at all. Teachers who were already showing up worked harder and taught better. Teachers who were in the habit of skipping school kept skipping school, because nothing in the bonus formula touched whether they walked through the gate in the morning. The incentive was engineered around test scores, and it moved test scores. It said nothing about attendance, and attendance simply refused to move. That gap is the principal agent problem made visible in a single dataset, across five hundred real schools with real children in them.

Now let us name the mistakes, because knowing a framework and applying it correctly under pressure are two very different skills.

The first trap is treating every price cut as automatically good for business, because it feels generous and moves inventory quickly. Volume moving is not the same as profit rising. Without an honest estimate of elasticity, a price cut is a hope dressed up as a strategy, and as the bookshop example showed, a modest looking cut can need a startlingly large volume response just to break even.

The second trap is confusing average cost with variable cost when deciding whether to shut something down. Managers see total cost divided by units exceeds the price, and conclude the business is a lost cause. But the shutdown decision only cares about variable cost. A business can be losing money overall on paper and still be exactly right to keep running, because every unit sold is shrinking the loss, not growing it.

The third trap, and perhaps the most universally human one, is sunk cost thinking, dressed up in respectable language. Nobody says I am refusing to quit because I cannot admit the money is gone. They say we have come too far to stop now, or think of everything we have invested. Both sentences describe the exact same error. The money is gone regardless of the decision. Only the future numbers should decide what happens next.

The fourth trap is moral outrage substituting for economic reasoning when prices move during a shortage. It is entirely reasonable to be angry about paying more for a ride home, or for water during a disaster. But anger is not analysis. Before concluding that a surging price is simply exploitation, ask what would have happened to the queue and the available supply under a price that had stayed fixed. Often, the answer is that fewer people would have gotten what they needed, not more.

And the fifth trap, the one that catches even experienced managers, is designing an incentive around whatever is easiest to measure, rather than what actually matters. Test scores are easy to measure, attendance is harder to track consistently, so pay gets tied to the easy number, and the harder, equally important one quietly drifts. The lesson is not that incentives are bad. It is that every incentive is a bet on what you chose to measure, and people will take that bet completely literally.

Let us sprint back through all six tools before we close.

Elasticity tells you whether a price change earns you more money or less, by comparing the percentage swing in volume against the percentage swing in price. The Lerner index tells you that your markup is capped not by your ambition but by how easily your customer can walk away. The shutdown rule tells you to keep running as long as price covers variable cost, because something toward the rent beats nothing, and to stop the moment it does not. Sunk cost discipline tells you that money already spent has no vote in what you do next, only the future numbers matter. Price as a rationing device tells you that a rising price during a shortage is quietly doing two jobs, filtering demand and summoning supply, both at once. And the principal agent problem tells you that people will optimise precisely for what you measure, and quietly abandon whatever you forgot to include.

Your one sentence for microeconomics, prices are information, structure beats effort, sunk costs are silent, and every incentive you design will be obeyed more literally than you intended.
