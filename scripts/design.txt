The product team has been in the same conference room for six straight weeks, and the whiteboard along the far wall is covered in sketches of an app nobody outside this room has ever seen, a meal planning application for busy parents, built around a beautifully designed weekly calendar, drag and drop recipe cards, and a grocery list that automatically assembles itself from whatever meals get dragged into which day. The team is proud of it, genuinely proud, the kind of pride that comes from long hours and real craft, and the lead designer has spent an entire weekend polishing the onboarding animation until the little cartoon chef bounces exactly right when a new user opens the app for the first time. They have a working prototype, a full one, every screen built, every interaction smooth, ready to show a small group of actual parents recruited for a Thursday afternoon testing session.

The first parent who sits down with the prototype is a mother of two young children, home from work an hour earlier than usual, and the facilitator hands her a tablet and asks her to plan next week's dinners using the app. She opens it, sees the beautiful calendar, sees the recipe cards, and after about ninety seconds of scrolling, sets the tablet down on the table and says something the team was not prepared to hear. She says this looks lovely, but she does not actually know what to cook tonight, she never really knows, and a beautiful calendar full of recipe options does not solve that problem, it makes it slightly worse, because now she has to choose from an even larger menu of possibilities on a Tuesday evening when she is exhausted, her children are asking when dinner will be ready, and the last thing in the world she wants is more choices to sort through. What she actually wants, she says, almost apologetically, as though she is disappointing the team by not loving their calendar, is someone to just tell her what to make, quickly, with ingredients she probably already has, so she can stop thinking about it and start cooking.

The room goes quiet. Six weeks of sketches, a beautifully animated cartoon chef, an elegant drag and drop calendar, all of it built around an assumption nobody had actually tested until this exact Thursday afternoon, the assumption that the barrier standing between a tired parent and a good weeknight dinner was a lack of organised recipe options. It was never that. The barrier was decision fatigue, the sheer mental exhaustion of having to choose anything at all after a full day of choosing things for other people, and every single design decision the team had made for six weeks had been quietly built on top of the wrong problem entirely. The lead designer, staring at her carefully animated onboarding screen, realises in that moment that she had fallen in love with her solution weeks before she had ever properly fallen in love with the actual problem sitting in front of her, and that the two are not the same thing at all, however easy they are to confuse when the whiteboard is full and the prototype finally works.

Hello, and welcome to the Decision Notebook listening library. Today, design thinking, a method built almost entirely around preventing exactly the mistake that meal planning team made, the seductive, easy mistake of building something impressive before you have properly understood what anyone actually needs. Ahead of us, the five stages of the method and what each one specifically protects you from, the discipline of writing a problem statement that never once mentions a solution, the reason rough, unfinished prototypes teach you more than polished ones ever will, and the three lens test that asks whether people want a thing, whether you can build it, and whether it makes money, in that exact order and never any other. Now here is the one thread underneath everything this course teaches, worth holding onto through every framework that follows. Teams do not usually fail at design thinking because they lack creativity, or lack resources, or lack talented people in the room. They fail because they fall in love with their solution when they should be falling in love with the problem. Once a team has sketched something, built something, named something, that thing starts to feel real, and feeling real is seductive, it generates pride, momentum, a sense of progress that a blank problem statement never gives you. The entire method this course teaches is, underneath its five neat stages, a set of deliberate speed bumps designed to slow that seduction down, to force a team to stay curious about the problem for longer than feels comfortable, precisely because the problem, examined honestly and patiently, is where the real insight almost always lives, while the solution, however early and however exciting it feels, is usually just the first guess everyone in the room happened to have in common.

The method itself has five stages, and it is worth naming them plainly before we take them one at a time. Empathise, define, ideate, prototype, and test. But hear this clearly, the stages themselves are not the insight of this course. The insight is what each stage specifically protects you from, and that is how we will work through them, one danger at a time.

Let us begin with empathise, and the specific failure it exists to prevent, which is designing for an imaginary user who lives only in your own head.

In plain words, empathise means watching real people struggle with a real problem in their real, everyday context, rather than relying on what those people say about their own behaviour in a survey or an interview room. This distinction matters more than it sounds like it should, because what people say about themselves and what people actually do are frequently, quietly, different truths, not because people are lying, but because most of us are simply poor narrators of our own habits, especially habits we have practiced so long we no longer notice them happening at all.

The everyday version of this gap is something almost everyone has experienced from the other side, watching someone insist confidently that they always read instructions carefully before assembling furniture, and then observing that exact same person, minutes later, tossing the instruction booklet aside and simply working from the picture on the box. Neither statement was really a lie. The person genuinely believes they are a careful reader of instructions, because that is the kind of person they want to be, but their actual behaviour, watched rather than asked about, tells a more accurate and considerably more useful story for anyone trying to design a better set of furniture instructions.

The business example the course returns to often is a ticket booking application meant to serve a wide range of users, including elderly users who did not grow up with smartphones the way younger generations did. A team could interview an older relative about how she books train tickets and hear a confident, reasonably articulate account of her process. But watch her actually attempt to book a ticket on the app itself, sitting beside her rather than asking her afterward to describe it, and an entirely different, more useful story emerges, the exact screen where her thumb hesitates, the moment she squints at a button too small to read comfortably, the instant she gives up entirely and calls her grandson to finish the booking for her over the phone while she reads the details aloud to him. None of that hesitation, that squint, that eventual phone call, would ever surface in an interview, because users have adapted to their problems so completely, over months and years of quiet workarounds, that they genuinely cannot describe those problems to you anymore, the workaround has become invisible to them, absorbed into what simply feels like normal behaviour. Observation recovers exactly what interviews cannot, because it captures the moment of struggle itself, live, before the user has had time to explain it away or forget it happened at all.

When do you reach for this framework. At the very start of any design project, before a single sketch exists, and specifically whenever you notice a team relying entirely on what users say about themselves rather than watching what users actually do. The signal to watch for is a gap between a confident verbal account and an observed struggle, and that gap, once you learn to notice it, is where nearly every genuinely useful design insight is hiding.

So remember. What people say in an interview and what people do at the kitchen counter, or on the train ticket app, are frequently different truths, users have adapted to their own problems so thoroughly that they can no longer describe them, and watching a real person struggle in their real context recovers insight that no amount of careful questioning ever will.

Now define, the stage where the course insists the entire project is genuinely won or lost, because it is the exact place where teams either capture the real problem or quietly, without quite noticing, slip a favourite solution in disguised as a need.

In plain words, define means writing a single sentence with exactly three parts, the user, the need, and the insight. Commuting parents need fast weeknight dinners, because the real barrier is decision fatigue, not cooking time. Look closely at what that sentence structure actually forbids, because the discipline lives entirely in what it refuses to allow. It forbids naming a technology, a feature, or a specific solution anywhere inside it. Users need an app is not a genuine need statement at all, it is a solution wearing a need's clothing, smuggled in under the grammar of a need, and the moment a problem statement names a technology or a specific product form, the entire space of possible solutions has already been foreclosed before any real thinking has begun.

The everyday version of this trap is something most people have caught themselves doing in far smaller decisions. Imagine telling a friend you need a bigger car, when what you actually need is simply more space to transport your growing family comfortably on weekend trips. A bigger car is one possible solution to that underlying need, but naming the car as the need itself quietly closes off every other possibility, a roof rack, a trailer, planning trips differently, borrowing a vehicle occasionally, before any of those alternatives ever get the chance to be considered at all.

The business example with numbers is precisely the meal planning story from our opening, examined now through the lens of this specific discipline. The team's real, if unspoken, problem statement had effectively been something like busy parents need a well organised recipe and calendar app, a statement that already contains its own solution baked directly into the need, foreclosing the entire space of thinking before the six weeks of sketching even began. The correct problem statement, the one the team should have written before touching a single screen, reads instead, commuting parents need fast weeknight dinners, because the real barrier is decision fatigue, not cooking time. Notice what that correct version does differently, it names the user precisely, commuting parents rather than parents in general, it states the need in terms of an outcome, fast weeknight dinners, rather than a feature, and it carries an insight, the barrier is decision fatigue rather than cooking time itself, an insight the team could only have discovered by properly empathising first, watching a real tired parent struggle with an overwhelming menu of choices rather than a shortage of good recipes. A team working from that correctly written sentence would likely never have built an elegant, choice heavy calendar at all, because the insight itself points toward something almost opposite, fewer choices presented more confidently, perhaps a single suggested meal each evening rather than an entire week of options to sort through.

When do you reach for this discipline. Every single time a project begins, before any sketching, before any prototyping, and specifically, insist on it whenever you notice a problem statement drifting toward naming a feature, a platform, or a specific technology. Read the statement back and ask plainly, does this sentence survive if I delete every specific product idea from it and replace it only with the user, the outcome they need, and the insight explaining why the obvious barrier is not the real one. If the sentence collapses into meaninglessness once the solution is removed, it was never really a need statement, it was a solution wearing one.

So remember. A correct problem statement names the user precisely, states the need as an outcome rather than a feature, and carries an insight explaining the real barrier, and the moment your problem statement names a technology or a specific solution, you have skipped the thinking the define stage exists specifically to force.

Pause here and try this yourself. A hospital wants to reduce the number of patients who miss their follow up appointments. The team's first draft problem statement reads, patients need a better appointment reminder app. What is wrong with this statement using the discipline just described, and what kind of research would help rewrite it properly.

Here is the answer talked through. The statement names a solution, an app, directly inside what is supposed to be a need, which forecloses every other possible answer, a phone call from a nurse, a printed card with a large clear date, a family member notified directly, before any of them have been considered. The fix requires going back to empathise properly, observing why patients actually miss appointments in the first place rather than assuming the barrier is simply forgetting, because the real insight might turn out to be something else entirely, perhaps transportation difficulty on the appointment day, or confusion about which specific department to visit, or a fear of bad news that makes patients quietly avoid appointments rather than genuinely forget them. Only after that observation could the team write a properly disciplined statement, something closer to, patients living far from the hospital need confidence they can reach their follow up appointment reliably, because the real barrier is unpredictable transportation on the appointment day, not forgetfulness, a statement that opens up an entirely different, and possibly far more effective, set of solutions than another reminder app ever could.

Now ideate, the stage that exists specifically to protect a team from its own first idea, which is, almost without exception, the single most obvious idea in the room, the one everyone else independently thought of too.

In plain words, ideation means deliberately going wide before going deep, generating a large volume of possible solutions without judging or narrowing any of them prematurely, because the genuinely useful, non obvious ideas tend to arrive only after the obvious ones have been exhausted and cleared out of the way first. Deferring judgement is the specific discipline here, resisting the very natural, very strong urge to critique or dismiss an idea the moment it is spoken aloud, because that instinct to judge early is precisely what keeps a room stuck circling its first few obvious ideas rather than pushing further into stranger, more interesting territory.

The everyday version of this discipline is brainstorming names for a new family pet, where the first few suggestions that arrive are almost always the most common, predictable names anyone could think of, and it is only after pushing well past those first obvious suggestions, refusing to settle immediately, that the genuinely memorable, unusual name eventually surfaces, the one that actually captures something specific and true about this particular animal rather than being generically applicable to any pet at all.

The business example belongs to the meal planning team once more, now armed with their corrected problem statement about decision fatigue rather than recipe organisation. Their first instinct, even with the better problem statement in hand, was still to sketch another version of a calendar, simply a sparser one with fewer visible options at once. Pushed to keep generating past that first obvious idea, the team eventually arrived somewhere considerably more interesting, a single suggested meal delivered each morning with no menu of alternatives shown at all unless the parent specifically asked to see other options, essentially removing the choice entirely rather than merely organising it more neatly, an idea that would never have surfaced if the team had stopped generating the moment their first, familiar, calendar shaped idea appeared and felt comfortable enough to run with.

When do you reach for this discipline. Whenever a team's brainstorming session produces a handful of ideas and then quietly stalls, everyone in the room nodding along to whichever option feels safest or most familiar. Push past that stall deliberately, insist on a higher volume of ideas than feels natural, and hold off on judging any of them until the well of obvious answers has genuinely run dry.

So remember. Ideation protects a team from its own first idea, which is almost always the obvious one everyone else in the room independently had too, and the discipline is to go wide before going deep, deferring judgement long enough that quantity has the chance to breed something genuinely non obvious.

Now prototype and test together, because they share a single underlying purpose, protecting a team from the specific danger of expensive certainty, building something polished and complete before anyone outside the team has had the chance to react to it honestly.

In plain words, a prototype is a question, not a finished product, and the goal at this stage is to build the cheapest possible artefact that can still generate an honest reaction from a real user, a rough sketch on paper, a simple landing page nobody can actually purchase from yet, even a person sitting behind a curtain manually doing what the eventual software is meant to automate, anything that lets you learn quickly and cheaply before committing serious resources to building the real thing.

There is a genuine fidelity ladder here worth understanding explicitly, running from the roughest paper sketch, through simple clickable digital mockups that look real but do nothing underneath the surface, up to a fully working prototype indistinguishable from a finished product. The temptation, especially for skilled, proud teams, is to climb that ladder far higher and far faster than the question being asked actually requires, and this is exactly the trap the course flags most directly and most emphatically, because polish breeds attachment in a very specific and costly psychological way. A team that has spent eight weeks perfecting a prototype has also spent eight weeks growing attached to it, personally and professionally invested in its details, its animations, its careful visual choices, and that attachment changes how the team behaves the moment real users sit down to test it. Instead of genuinely listening and learning from what goes wrong, an attached team unconsciously begins defending the prototype, explaining away confusion, dismissing hesitation as an unusual user rather than a real design signal, protecting the thing they built rather than the problem they were originally trying to solve.

The everyday version of this trap is showing a friend a rough, hand drawn sketch of a birthday cake idea versus showing them a fully baked, beautifully decorated final cake, and asking, in either case, what they honestly think. Faced with the rough sketch, a friend feels entirely comfortable saying the colours feel wrong, or the shape looks awkward, because a sketch clearly signals that nothing is finished yet, and criticism at this stage is genuinely welcome, genuinely useful. Faced with the finished, beautifully decorated cake, the same honest friend will very often simply say it looks lovely, because criticising something that already looks complete and polished feels socially awkward, even cruel, regardless of what that friend privately thinks about the actual flavour or design. Rough prototypes invite criticism. Beautiful, finished looking ones invite compliments instead, and compliments teach a team almost nothing useful about whether the underlying idea genuinely works.

The business example with numbers returns, fittingly, to the meal planning team's revised idea, the single suggested meal with no visible menu of alternatives. Rather than spending another six weeks building a fully polished version of this new concept, complete with fresh onboarding animations and a redesigned interface, the team instead built the roughest possible version imaginable, a person on the team texting a single suggested dinner recipe each morning to a small group of volunteer parents, along with a simple grocery list, with absolutely no app involved at all. This entire prototype cost the team almost nothing beyond a few hours of someone's time each morning for two weeks, and it generated exactly the kind of honest, unguarded reactions a polished prototype rarely produces, several parents mentioned they actually wanted slightly more control over which single meal got suggested each day, based on what ingredients they already happened to have at home, an insight the team folded directly into the eventual real product, discovered for the cost of a few text messages rather than the cost of another six week sprint building the wrong feature in polished, defensible detail.

When do you reach for this discipline. At every single stage of a project where a real question still needs answering, and specifically, resist climbing the fidelity ladder any higher than the current question genuinely requires. If the question is simply whether an idea's core concept resonates at all, a rough sketch or an entirely manual, human powered stand in answers it just as well as a fully built application would, at a tiny fraction of the cost, and with considerably less risk of the team falling in love with its own polish before the real feedback has even arrived.

So remember. A prototype exists to ask a question, not to demonstrate a finished product, polish breeds attachment and attachment makes a team defend its work instead of learning from it, rough prototypes invite the honest criticism you actually need while beautiful ones invite only comfortable compliments, and the discipline is to build at the lowest fidelity that still answers the specific question currently in front of you.

Pause here and try this yourself. A team wants to test whether customers would actually pay for a subscription box of surprise books curated to their personal taste. One team member proposes spending two months building a fully functional website with payment processing and a recommendation algorithm before showing it to anyone. Using the fidelity ladder and the polish breeds attachment idea, what would a better first prototype look like, and why.

Here is the answer talked through. The two month fully functional website answers a question that does not actually need to be asked yet, whether the payment processing and recommendation algorithm work correctly, when the real, more urgent question is simply whether anyone wants a surprise curated book subscription at all. A far better first prototype is a single, simple web page describing the offer clearly, with a button that says something like reserve your spot, collecting email addresses and even a small deposit from genuinely interested people without any actual books, algorithm, or subscription infrastructure existing behind it yet. This kind of lean prototype can be built in days rather than months, and because it is visibly rough and clearly not a finished product, people who see it will give an honest reaction rather than a polite one, telling the team plainly whether the core idea excites them or leaves them cold, well before two months and a serious budget have been spent building payment infrastructure for an idea nobody may have actually wanted in the first place.

Now, above the five stages sits a single test that governs the entire method, worth treating as its own framework because so many otherwise well run design projects fail specifically by getting its order wrong. The three lens test, desirability, feasibility, and viability.

In plain words, desirability asks whether people genuinely want the thing being designed. Feasibility asks whether it is realistically possible to build, given available technology, skill, and time. Viability asks whether it can actually make money, or otherwise sustain itself, once built. All three lenses must eventually hold true for a design to succeed in the real world, but the sequence in which a team applies them is the entire point of this framework, and getting that sequence backward is one of the single most expensive mistakes an organisation can make.

The everyday version of this sequencing problem is planning an elaborate surprise party for a friend who has, on several previous occasions, mentioned clearly that they genuinely dislike surprises and large social gatherings. A team of friends could spend weeks perfecting the feasibility of this surprise, booking a venue, coordinating dozens of guests, arranging catering, solving every logistical problem with real skill and care, and could even make the viability work out financially within budget, and still produce an outcome that makes the supposed guest of honour genuinely miserable, because nobody stopped early enough to properly check the desirability question first, whether this particular friend actually wants a surprise party at all.

The business example with numbers is exactly why the course teaches this sequence so insistently, desirability first, because feasibility and viability analysis applied carefully and skilfully to something nobody actually wants produces, reliably, a well engineered, profitable looking plan on paper that fails completely in the market, and the market has an endless supply of exactly this particular kind of failure, technically excellent, financially sound on paper, and simply unwanted. A well resourced team could spend a genuinely large budget building a technically sophisticated, feasible product, hit every engineering milestone on schedule, produce viable unit economics on a spreadsheet showing healthy margins at scale, and launch to near silence, because the desirability question, does anyone actually want this specific thing, was never properly tested with real users early enough to catch the problem before the serious money had already been spent building it.

When do you reach for this framework. At every major decision point in a project, and specifically, insist on checking desirability first, before feasibility or viability analysis consumes significant time and budget, because a team that has already invested heavily in proving something is buildable and profitable becomes strongly, understandably motivated to find evidence that people want it too, rather than genuinely, openly testing whether they do.

So remember. Desirability, feasibility, and viability must all eventually hold for a design to succeed, but the sequence matters enormously, desirability first, because skilled, careful analysis applied to an unwanted idea produces nothing more than a well engineered, profitable looking failure, and the market has never once run short of those.

Let us sit inside one fuller story now, because the discipline of checking desirability before anything else is easiest to remember once you have watched a well resourced, genuinely capable organisation get the sequence badly wrong in public.

In the middle of two thousand eleven, a major streaming and DVD rental company announced a strategic decision that made a great deal of sense on paper, and had clearly been through serious feasibility and viability analysis before anyone announced it publicly. The company had been running two genuinely different businesses under one roof for years, a mailed DVD rental service with its own distinct logistics, costs, and customer base, and a streaming service growing quickly with an entirely different technology stack and cost structure altogether. From a pure operational and financial logic standpoint, separating these two businesses into distinct companies made real sense, each one could be run, priced, and optimised according to its own specific economics, without one business's constraints quietly dragging on the other's decisions. The company announced it would split the streaming service and the DVD rental service into two separate offerings, with the DVD business spun into its own distinct brand and website entirely, requiring customers who wanted both services to now manage two separate accounts, two separate logins, and two separate monthly bills instead of the single unified account they had used comfortably for years. The plan was announced with genuine confidence, the kind of confidence that comes from having run the numbers carefully and found them sound.

Customers did not experience this decision as elegant operational logic at all. They experienced it as their familiar, convenient service suddenly and inexplicably becoming more complicated and more annoying to use, exactly the opposite direction any reasonable customer wants their favourite service to move in. The backlash arrived quickly and loudly, across social media, in the press, in a wave of subscriber cancellations that began almost immediately and showed no sign of slowing. The company had clearly done the feasibility work, the new structure could genuinely be built and operated as described, and had clearly done the viability work too, the separated economics likely did make longer term financial sense exactly as the internal analysis suggested. What had never been properly tested, in the way this course insists it must be tested, first and separately from the other two lenses, was desirability, whether the company's actual customers, living their actual daily lives, wanted to manage two separate accounts and two separate bills for a service they had always experienced, and genuinely valued, as one single, simple thing. Within a matter of weeks, facing a subscriber exodus that showed no sign of stabilising, the company reversed the decision entirely, walking the split back and reuniting the two services under one account exactly as they had existed before the announcement, a strategically sensible plan on paper, unwound in public, in a matter of weeks, because the desirability question had never been asked of the people who actually mattered, the customers who would have to live with the change every single day.

Now the traps, because design thinking has a particular set of misconceptions that catch smart, capable teams surprisingly often, usually teams with real skill and real resources who simply moved too fast past a stage they assumed was optional.

The first trap is relying on what users say about themselves in an interview rather than watching what they actually do, missing the gap between confident self description and observed, honest struggle.

The second trap is writing a problem statement that quietly names a solution, a technology, or a specific feature, foreclosing an entire space of better answers before any real thinking has had the chance to begin.

The third trap is stopping the ideation stage the moment the first comfortable, familiar idea appears, never pushing far enough past the obvious to reach the genuinely non obvious answer waiting further along.

The fourth trap is building a prototype at a far higher fidelity than the current question actually requires, and growing personally attached to that polish in a way that quietly discourages the team from hearing honest criticism when it finally arrives.

The fifth trap is applying feasibility and viability analysis before desirability has been properly tested, producing, reliably, a well engineered plan that works beautifully on paper and fails quietly, or loudly, the moment real customers encounter it.

And the sixth trap, perhaps the hardest one to notice from inside a project, is treating strategy or operational logic as sufficient justification on its own, forgetting that a plan which ignores how real people will actually experience a change is not really strategy at all, it is hope wearing an organisational chart.

Let us close with the revision sprint. Empathise means watching real people struggle in their real context rather than trusting what they say about themselves, because users have adapted so completely to their own problems that they can no longer describe them. Define is where projects are genuinely won or lost, and the discipline is a single sentence naming the user, the need as an outcome, and the insight explaining the real barrier, never a technology or a solution. Ideate protects a team from its own obvious first idea by going wide before going deep and deferring judgement long enough for quantity to breed something genuinely non obvious. Prototype and test protect a team from expensive certainty, building only at the fidelity the current question requires, because rough work invites the honest criticism you need while polished work invites only comfortable compliments. And the three lens test of desirability, feasibility, and viability must be checked in that exact order, because skilled analysis applied to something nobody wants produces nothing more than a well engineered, profitable looking failure.

Your one sentence for design thinking is this. Watch before you define, define the need and never the solution, build rough so you can learn cheap, and check that anyone genuinely wants the thing before you engineer it beautifully and profitably.
